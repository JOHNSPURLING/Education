## At a glance

- Published by the Education Endowment Foundation in June 2021; it is non-statutory advice, not a DfE or Ofsted requirement.
- Six recommendations: three on the principles of effective feedback, two on methods (written and verbal), one on school policy.
- The report's central claim is that method (written or verbal) is likely to matter less than following the principles in recommendations 1–3.
- The EEF Toolkit rates feedback at +6 months' additional progress on average for very low cost, but effects vary widely and some studies find negative effects.
- The practical test for any feedback: do pupils get time to use it, and does their work or understanding change as a result?

## What the evidence says

The report draws on a systematic review of feedback studies plus consultation with teachers. Its recommendations, in summary:

1. **Lay the foundations.** Feedback only works on top of good initial teaching and formative assessment that shows where pupils actually are. Feedback cannot repair poor instruction.
2. **Deliver appropriately timed feedback that moves learning forward.** There is no single best timing (immediate or delayed); the teacher judges this against the task, the pupil and the class. Feedback should focus on the task, the subject or the pupil's self-regulation strategies, not on the person ("you're a natural at this") and not on vague general remarks.
3. **Plan for how pupils will receive and use feedback.** Motivation, self-confidence and trust in the teacher affect whether feedback lands. Pupils need an opportunity to act on it.
4. **Use written feedback carefully.** Written comments can work if they follow the principles, but they are costly in teacher time; the opportunity cost should be weighed.
5. **Use verbal feedback carefully.** It is time-efficient but must be just as purposeful as written feedback, not a stream of general praise.
6. **Design a school feedback policy** that prioritises and exemplifies the principles rather than specifying frequency or method.

Not verified: the exact wording of recommendations 3 and 5 could not be checked against the EEF page on the day of writing (the site was not reachable); the summaries above are in our own words and consistent with Research School summaries.

The Toolkit feedback strand reports an average of +6 months for very low cost. Search results for the live page describe the evidence as "extensive", and suggest verbal feedback studies show slightly higher average effects (+7 months); older EEF materials describe the evidence as moderate. Check the live page before quoting the padlock rating.

## In the classroom

- **Before feedback, check the teaching.** If most of a Year 9 class has misread a graph question in science, the response is reteaching, not thirty individual comments.
- **Aim feedback at the task, subject or self-regulation.** History: "Your second paragraph describes the Treaty; add a sentence that explains *why* it caused resentment." Maths: "Check line 3: what happens to the sign when you multiply both sides by a negative?" Self-regulation: "Which of your success criteria did you check before handing this in?"
- **Build in response time.** Ten minutes at the start of the next lesson for redrafting one paragraph, re-attempting three questions or correcting a labelled diagram. Feedback with no response task is usually wasted.
- **Choose timing deliberately.** Immediate correction suits a new procedure (balancing equations, verb endings in MFL); delayed feedback can suit extended writing where pupils benefit from attempting the whole task first.
- **Keep it manageable.** Codes, whole-class feedback and live marking can all meet the principles at lower cost than extended written comments.

## For leaders

- Rewrite feedback policies around principles, and let departments define what those look like in their subject. PE, art, maths and English should not be held to one format.
- Remove frequency rules ("every two weeks"), colour-coding rules and requirements for written evidence of verbal feedback; none is supported by the report.
- Monitor by looking at whether pupils' work improves after feedback and by talking to pupils, not by counting comments in books.
- Treat teacher time as the main cost. The Toolkit's "very low cost" refers to financial cost, mostly training; it does not price the hours spent marking.
- Follow the EEF implementation guidance: introduce changes gradually, with exemplification and training, rather than by issuing a new policy document.

## Cautions and critiques

- The +6 months average hides large variation, including negative effects. An average across many different interventions does not guarantee that a given school's marking practice works.
- Much of the underlying research is short-term, in particular subjects, or from outside English secondary schools. The EEF's earlier review of written marking, 'A Marked Improvement?' (2016), found very little robust evidence on specific marking practices.
- Some commentators argue the recommendations are sensible but general, leaving most of the real decisions to teacher judgement. That is arguably a faithful reflection of the evidence rather than a flaw.
- "Feedback" in the research includes much more than marking; schools should not read the Toolkit figure as an endorsement of heavy written marking.
