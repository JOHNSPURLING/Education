## At a glance

- A hinge question is asked at a planned "hinge point" in a lesson; the answers decide what the teacher does next.
- A diagnostic question is any question designed so that each wrong answer points to a particular misconception. A good hinge question is a diagnostic question used at the right moment.
- Every pupil must answer (mini-whiteboards, fingers, cards, devices), quickly, and the teacher must be able to read the responses at a glance.
- Writing good ones is hard and subject-specific: it is best done collaboratively in departments and banked.
- The technique is well reasoned and widely used, but there is no separate body of trial evidence for hinge questions as such.

## What the evidence says

The idea comes from Dylan Wiliam's work on formative assessment (strategy two: engineering tasks that elicit evidence). His design tests, as usually summarised: pupils should be able to answer in about a minute or less; the teacher should be able to interpret the class's responses in seconds; and it should be very difficult to get the right answer for the wrong reason. Wiliam's 2015 article 'Designing Great Hinge Questions' in Educational Leadership is the main primary statement (not opened for this entry).

Craig Barton developed the approach for mathematics and, with Eedi, built a large free bank at diagnosticquestions.com. His "golden rules" add that the question should be clear and unambiguous, test a single skill or concept, be answerable in under ten seconds or so, and have wrong answers from which you learn something specific without needing the pupil to explain. He prefers not to call wrong options "distractors", since they are not there to trick.

The evidence base is indirect: research on formative assessment generally, on the value of surfacing misconceptions (particularly in science and maths), and on multiple-choice questions as retrieval practice. One question is a small sample of a pupil's understanding and can mislead; the Chartered College guide makes this point.

## In the classroom

**Routine**

1. Plan the hinge point: the moment after the core explanation and before independent practice.
2. Display the question. Silent thinking time; no conferring.
3. "Three, two, one, show me." All pupils answer simultaneously.
4. Scan, then act: nearly all correct, move on; a significant minority wrong, ask a pupil who chose each option to explain and reteach briefly; most wrong, reteach differently.
5. Follow with a second, similar question to check the reteach worked.

**Examples**

- *Maths (Year 8):* Which is the largest: A 0.5, B 0.45, C 0.405, D 0.09? Choosing B or C suggests "longer decimals are bigger".
- *Science (Year 9):* A ball is thrown upwards. At the top of its flight the forces acting on it are: A none; B an upward force and gravity, balanced; C gravity only; D an upward force only. A and B reveal the "force needed for motion" and "stationary means balanced" misconceptions.
- *English (Year 10):* Which sentence analyses rather than describes the writer's choice? Four candidate sentences about the same quotation.
- *History (Year 9):* Which statement is a consequence, not a cause, of the 1832 Reform Act?
- *MFL:* Choose the correct translation of "I went": options isolating auxiliary choice and past-participle agreement.

**Writing them**

- Start from pupils' actual wrong answers in books, mocks and exam board examiner reports.
- One concept per question; plausible wrong answers of similar length; avoid "all of the above".
- Trial with one class, then refine in the department meeting.

## For leaders

- Give departments meeting time to write and bank questions against each unit in the scheme of work. This is curriculum work with lasting value and low cost.
- Supply mini-whiteboards and pens as standard classroom kit, and expect routines for using them.
- Online platforms (Eedi, quiz tools in the school's learning platform) can collect responses and show misconception patterns; check data protection and avoid turning them into a data drop.
- In lesson visits, look for the response to the answers, not merely the presence of a question.

## Cautions and critiques

- **Guessing.** With four options a quarter of pupils with no idea will be right. Barton suggests asking for a reason or using follow-up questions; pairs of questions help.
- **Single-item inference is weak.** A correct answer does not show secure, durable learning; performance in the lesson is not the same as learning over time.
- **Not everything is multiple-choice.** Extended reasoning, practical skill and creative work need other checks.
- **Poor options waste the technique.** Implausible wrong answers tell the teacher nothing.
- **Time cost of writing.** Without shared banks, teachers revert to ad hoc questioning.
- Some commentators note hinge questions are largely a convention of British CPD rather than an intervention with its own research literature; treat claims of proven impact with caution.
