## At a glance

- Levels were removed with the 2014 national curriculum. There is no statutory assessment at Key Stage 3 at present and no prescribed in-school system.
- The Commission on Assessment Without Levels (chaired by John McIntosh, final report September 2015) gave principles, not a template: assessment should follow the curriculum, and schools should not rebuild levels.
- The most common secondary response, GCSE grades 1–9 applied from Year 7, is widely criticised as invalid.
- The stronger model: specify what pupils should know and be able to do in each unit, test that, and report scores against the year group.
- Change is coming: after the Curriculum and Assessment Review (November 2025), the government announced a statutory Year 8 reading test and an expectation that schools assess writing and maths in Year 8. Check the latest DfE position before planning.

## What the evidence says

**Why levels went.** The Commission and the earlier expert panel argued that levels:

- encouraged pace through content over secure understanding;
- relied on "best fit" judgements that hid serious gaps, so two pupils at the same level could know very different things;
- were used for incompatible purposes (formative feedback, tracking, accountability), distorting each;
- labelled pupils, who came to see themselves as "a level 4".

**What the Commission advised.** Decide the purpose of each assessment first. Keep formative assessment for teaching; do not log it in tracking systems. Make in-school summative assessment a periodic check on what has been taught. Avoid systems that count objectives ticked or assign pupils to bands at frequent intervals. The government accepted the report.

**The 'curriculum is the progression model' argument.** Associated with Michael Fordham and Christine Counsell, and with Daisy Christodoulou's 'Making Good Progress?' (2017): if the curriculum is well sequenced, making progress means learning that curriculum. Assessment should therefore check the specific content, using a "quality" model (judging complex performances such as essays) sparingly and a "difficulty" model (many specific questions) often. Christodoulou also argues that prose descriptors, whether levels or grade criteria, cannot define a standard reliably.

**What schools actually did.** NFER and others found many schools kept level-like systems: emerging/developing/secure/mastered bands, or GCSE grade flight paths. Not verified: the detailed NFER findings were not re-read for this entry.

## In the classroom

- **Unit specification.** For each Year 7–9 unit the department lists the core knowledge and the performances expected (for example in Year 8 geography: define and use 20 terms on rivers, explain three processes of erosion, interpret a hydrograph, write an extended answer on flood management).
- **Regular low-stakes checks** of the core knowledge: quizzes, hinge questions. Scores stay with the teacher.
- **Common summative assessments** once or twice a year, cumulative, mixing short-answer questions with one extended task. Report as a percentage alongside the year-group mean.
- **Extended performances** (essay, practical write-up, composition, artwork) judged against annotated exemplars from the department rather than a generic grid; comparative judgement can help moderate.
- **In practical subjects** (PE, music, DT, art), assess specified techniques and knowledge taught in the unit; avoid forcing a numerical scale onto half-termly lessons.
- **Reading and maths** tracked with a standardised test once a year gives a national reference point that subject tests cannot.

## For leaders

- Test any system with three questions: does it tell teachers what to reteach, does it tell parents something true and understandable, and what does it cost in time?
- Let subjects differ. A common reporting language (percentage and year average; or a small number of bands derived from test scores at the end of the year) is enough.
- Review Key Stage 3 assessments alongside curriculum plans in line management: are tests sampling what was taught, including earlier units?
- **Prepare for national change.** The government's response to the Curriculum and Assessment Review (5 November 2025) said it would introduce a statutory reading test in Year 8 and expect schools to assess writing and maths in Year 8; the Review itself had recommended diagnostic assessment in English and maths in Year 8. Not verified: the test's start date, format, and whether results will be published or used in accountability, as of November 2026. A revised national curriculum is planned for first teaching from September 2028.

## Cautions and critiques

- **GCSE grades at Key Stage 3.** Grades describe performance across a whole GCSE specification at 16. A Year 7 "grade 2" in history has no defined meaning, invites flight paths, and tells pupils with low prior attainment they are failing for years. Ofqual grade descriptors were never designed for this use.
- **Levels by another name.** "Emerging, developing, secure, mastery" bands, judged by best fit each half-term, reproduce the problems the Commission identified.
- **Tick-list tracking.** Recording hundreds of objectives per pupil as met or not met is unreliable and heavy on workload.
- **Loss of a common language.** Critics of the removal of levels point out that parents and receiving schools lost a shared scale and that schools were left to invent systems with little support; inconsistency between schools is the price of autonomy.
- **Percentages need context.** A raw 55% means little without the cohort average and some sense of test difficulty; tests vary in difficulty from year to year.
- **National testing concerns.** Unions and some commentators have warned that a statutory Year 8 test could narrow the Key Stage 3 curriculum or become an accountability measure.
