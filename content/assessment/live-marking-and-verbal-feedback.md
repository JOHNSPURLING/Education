## At a glance

- Live marking means reading and responding to pupils' work during the lesson: circulating with a pen, or putting a pupil's work under a visualiser for the whole class.
- Verbal feedback is the oldest and cheapest form of feedback. The EEF's 2021 guidance says it should be used carefully and purposefully, not as an easy substitute for marking.
- Its advantage is timing: pupils can act on it immediately, while still working on the task.
- Its risks are that it is thin ("good, keep going"), unevenly distributed, and forgotten.
- Do not require stamps or written logs of verbal feedback; the 2016 workload review criticised recording feedback for the benefit of observers.

## What the evidence says

The EEF Toolkit's feedback strand reports +6 months on average. Search results for the live strand page indicate that studies of verbal feedback show slightly higher average impact (+7 months) than the overall figure; this was not verified directly, and the difference should not be over-read given how varied the studies are.

The EEF guidance report (recommendation 5) describes verbal feedback as including brief in-the-moment comments, structured individual conferences and whole-class verbal feedback such as work under a visualiser. It applies the same tests as for any feedback: grounded in good teaching, targeted at the task, the subject or self-regulation rather than the person, and followed by pupil action. The report is clear that method matters less than these principles.

There is little trial evidence on "live marking" as a named practice. The case for it rests on feedback principles and on workload: the 2016 Marking Policy Review Group encouraged schools to consider in-lesson feedback as a way to make marking manageable.

## In the classroom

**Circulating with purpose**

- Set a substantial independent task (10–20 minutes) so there is time to circulate.
- Decide in advance what you are looking for, for example "units on every answer" in physics or "topic sentence answers the question" in history. Lemov calls this tracking, not watching.
- Walk a planned route; start with pupils most likely to struggle, and make sure every pupil is seen across a week (a seating-plan tick sheet helps).
- At each desk: read, name one thing, give an action, move on within 30 seconds. "Your method's right to line 2. Check the expansion in line 3, then redo it." Mark a dot or underline at the place to fix; pupil corrects in a different colour if the school uses one.
- Return to check the correction has been made.
- Keep a tally of repeated errors. When the same issue appears four or five times, stop the class and reteach.

**Visualiser feedback**

- Mid-task, take one pupil's work (with a class culture that makes this normal) and show it: "What has Amira done well in this evaluation? What one change would move it up?" Then all pupils check their own for the same feature.
- In art, DT, food and PE, the equivalent is a mid-lesson demonstration or peer performance with one coaching point.

**Structured conferences**

- For A-level essays, NEA planning (within exam board rules) or EPQ, a five-minute one-to-one conversation with the pupil noting their own targets can replace a page of written comment.

**Wording**

- Prefer questions and prompts that make the pupil think: "Which piece of evidence best supports that?" over supplying the answer.
- Avoid general praise as feedback. Be specific about what is right and why.

## For leaders

- Accept that live feedback leaves little trace in books. Policies should say so explicitly, and quality assurance should rely on lesson visits, conversations with pupils and improvement in work over time.
- Ban "VF" stamps and requirements for pupils to write down what the teacher said as proof.
- Provide visualisers in every classroom and train staff to use them fluently; cost is modest.
- Train the skills: task design that frees the teacher to circulate, what to look for, concise wording. Instructional coaching suits this well.
- Timetabling and class size affect feasibility: 30 pupils in a 50-minute lesson allows well under a minute each.

## Cautions and critiques

- **Uneven attention.** Without a plan, teachers gravitate to pupils who ask for help or who misbehave; quiet pupils with errors get missed.
- **Dependence.** Constant in-task correction can stop pupils thinking for themselves and can mask whether they could do the task unaided. Some practice should be done, and assessed, without help.
- **Performance versus learning.** Work corrected live looks good in the book; it does not show the pupil has retained the point. Check again later.
- **Quality control.** Verbal feedback is invisible to colleagues, so weak practice is hard to spot and good practice hard to share.
- **Not a full replacement.** Extended writing often needs reading in full, outside the lesson, to judge argument and structure.
