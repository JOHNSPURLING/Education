## At a glance

- There is no statutory requirement, and no Ofsted requirement, for any particular frequency, type or volume of marking. Marking policy is a school decision.
- The DfE's Independent Teacher Workload Review Group (March 2016) said all marking should be "meaningful, manageable and motivating" and criticised "deep" marking done for accountability rather than for pupils.
- The EEF/Oxford review 'A Marked Improvement?' (April 2016) found a striking lack of robust evidence on written marking, and concluded schools should mark less but mark better.
- Triple-impact marking, dialogic marking in multiple pen colours and "verbal feedback given" stamps have no good evidence behind them and a high time cost.
- A sound policy describes principles of feedback and trusts departments on method; it does not set a marking timetable.

## What the evidence says

**The workload review (2016).** The group, chaired by Dawn Copping, found that marking had become disproportionate in many schools, driven by a false belief that quantity of written comment equals quality, and by perceived inspection expectations. It defined the three tests: marking should be *meaningful* (it advances pupil progress and varies by age, subject and task), *manageable* (proportionate to the teacher's overall workload) and *motivating* (it helps pupils progress without the teacher doing more work than the pupil). It rejected extensive written dialogue and the practice of writing detailed comments mainly as evidence for observers.

**'A Marked Improvement?' (2016).** Elliott and colleagues at Oxford, for the EEF, found few large, robust studies of written marking. Their tentative findings:

- Careless slips are best simply flagged as wrong for the pupil to correct; errors from misunderstanding need a hint, question or reteaching.
- Awarding a grade on every piece may reduce the impact of comments, because pupils attend to the grade.
- "Acknowledgement" marking (ticks to show work was seen) is unlikely to improve progress.
- Specific, actionable targets are more useful than general ones, and pupils need set-aside time to respond.
- There was no evidence either way on triple-impact or dialogic marking; the review called for trials to test whether the time is justified.

**EEF guidance report (2021).** Written feedback can work when it follows the principles of good feedback, but its time cost should be weighed against alternatives such as verbal and whole-class feedback.

## In the classroom

- **Sort errors before responding.** In a set of Year 8 French paragraphs, a dot in the margin signals a slip the pupil can fix (missing accent, agreement); a recurring misunderstanding (perfect tense with *être*) gets five minutes of reteaching to everyone.
- **Use marking codes** agreed in the department (for example Sp, P, "?" for unclear, "Ev" for missing evidence) rather than sentences. Pupils do the correcting.
- **Mark selectively.** In English, give detailed written comment on one paragraph of an essay, or on the planned assessment piece each half-term, rather than light-touch comments on every page.
- **Comment-only on drafts; grade the final piece.** Hold back the mark until pupils have responded.
- **Make the pupil work harder than you.** A feedback task (redraft the conclusion, redo questions 4–6 with the corrected method) should take the pupil longer than the comment took to write.
- **Self-marking of practice work.** Maths and science classwork with right/wrong answers can be marked by pupils against a visualiser model, with the teacher circulating to spot patterns.

## For leaders

- Audit the hours. Ask staff how long marking takes per week per class; the opportunity cost is planning and curriculum work.
- Replace a marking policy with a feedback policy built on principles; let each department write a one-page statement of how feedback works in its subject and at which points extended work receives individual comment.
- Stop book scrutiny that checks compliance (frequency, pen colour, dated stamps). Look instead at whether pupils' later work shows improvement and whether pupils can explain what they are working on.
- Be explicit to staff, governors and parents that written comments are not being produced as evidence for inspection. Tell parents what feedback their child will receive instead, or less visible marking will be read as neglect.
- Marking is contractual work only within directed and reasonable additional hours; unions including NASUWT publish guidance drawing on the 2016 report.

## Cautions and critiques

- **Triple-impact marking** (teacher comments, pupil responds, teacher comments again) spread widely around 2012–2015, partly because written dialogue was visible to inspectors. The workload review treated it as an unhelpful fad; the EEF/Oxford review found no evidence base. The critique is about cost and compliance, not about pupils responding to feedback, which is sensible.
- Cutting marking is not the same as improving feedback. A "no written marking" rule can be as dogmatic as the policy it replaces; essays, NEA drafts (within exam board rules) and A-level work may warrant individual written comment.
- The evidence base is thin in both directions: there is little proof that heavy marking works, and little direct trial evidence on the alternatives.
- Ofsted's published clarifications from 2016 onwards stated that it does not expect any specific frequency, type or volume of marking. Not verified: the exact wording on marking in the inspection toolkit and operating guides in force since November 2025 was not checked for this entry; nothing found suggests the position has changed.
