## At a glance

- Self- and peer assessment are strategies four and five of Wiliam's five formative assessment strategies: pupils as resources for each other and as owners of their own learning.
- The purpose is learning, not marking. Pupils improve by internalising what good work looks like; the feedback they give each other is a secondary benefit.
- Novices judge quality poorly. Without models, criteria and training, peer comments are vague or wrong and self-assessment is over-optimistic.
- Best suited to checking against clear right/wrong answers or specific, observable criteria; least suited to holistic grading.
- Never use pupil-awarded marks for reporting, tracking or anything high-stakes.

## What the evidence says

- **Peer assessment.** A 2020 meta-analysis of control-group studies by Double, McGrane and Hopfenbeck reported a small-to-moderate positive average effect of peer assessment on attainment (around 0.3), compared with no assessment and with teacher assessment. Not verified: the paper was not opened for this entry; the figure is from memory of the published abstract and should be checked before being quoted.
- **Self-assessment.** Reviews (for example by Brown and Harris, and by Andrade) find that self-assessment tends to help when it is formative, criteria-based and followed by a chance to revise, and that accuracy is lower among younger and lower-attaining pupils. Novices overestimate their own performance, which is consistent with wider cognitive science findings on judgements of learning.
- **Metacognition.** The EEF Toolkit rates metacognition and self-regulation approaches as high impact; self-assessment is one component of these, not the whole. The EEF's EFA trial, which included peer and self-assessment among its five strategies, found a small positive effect on Attainment 8 (+2 months).
- **EEF feedback guidance (2021)** focuses on teacher feedback but notes that pupils need to be prepared to receive and use feedback, including being taught to monitor their own work.

## In the classroom

**Self-marking of closed tasks (lowest risk, immediate gain)**

- After a retrieval quiz in science or a set of maths questions, pupils mark their own in a different colour from answers displayed. Teacher asks for a show of scores or samples specific questions, then responds.
- Pupils write a correction, not just a cross.

**Checklists against specific criteria**

- English, Year 9 persuasive writing: "Underline your counter-argument. Circle three rhetorical devices and label them. If you can't find one, add it now."
- Geography: "Tick where you have used a named example and a figure."
- This is self-assessment as editing, with something to do at the end.

**Structured peer feedback**

1. Teach the standard first: compare two models and agree what makes one stronger.
2. Give a tight frame: one "kind, specific, helpful" comment per criterion, or "one thing done well with evidence; one precise next step".
3. Rehearse on an anonymous piece as a class before pupils assess each other.
4. The author must act: redraft the weakest section in response.
5. Teacher samples the peer comments to check quality.

**Practical and performance subjects**

- PE, drama, music and MFL speaking lend themselves to peer coaching with a single observation focus ("watch the plant foot", "listen for the past tense endings"), often with tablets for video replay.

**Exam classes**

- Pupils mark a past-paper answer with the mark scheme, then compare with the teacher's mark and discuss the difference. This teaches the mark scheme; it does not produce a trustworthy mark.

## For leaders

- Do not count peer or self-assessment as a marking substitute in policy ("one in three pieces peer-marked"). Position it as a teaching strategy.
- Agree departmental models and criteria so pupils meet consistent standards.
- Train staff in the set-up; done badly it wastes lesson time.
- Be alert to social dynamics: pairing, friendship bias and the exposure of pupils with SEND or low prior attainment. Anonymised or teacher-selected examples reduce risk.
- No internal data should be built on pupil-assigned grades.

## Cautions and critiques

- **Accuracy.** Pupils are unreliable judges of quality in complex tasks; peer marks correlate imperfectly with teacher marks and are inflated among friends.
- **The curse of the criteria.** Christodoulou argues that prose criteria and rubrics do not convey a standard; pupils (and teachers) need exemplars. Self-assessment against "level descriptors" was a weak legacy of the levels era.
- **Confidence ratings** (traffic lights, thumbs) measure feelings, not learning, and can mislead the teacher.
- **Opportunity cost.** Time spent on poor peer assessment could be spent on teacher instruction or practice.
- **Workload displacement.** The 2016 workload review cautioned against practices done to satisfy policy rather than help pupils; mandated peer-marking stickers and logs fall into this category.
