## At a glance

- Summative assessment sums up what a pupil has learned at a point in time: end-of-unit tests, end-of-year exams, mocks. Its results support reporting, intervention decisions and curriculum review.
- The Teacher Workload Advisory Group's 'Making data work' (November 2018, chaired by Becky Allen) said schools should not have more than two or three attainment data collection points a year, and that each should be used to act. The DfE and Ofsted accepted its recommendations.
- Collect data only where you know who will use it, for what decision, and that it is accurate enough for that decision.
- Half-termly "data drops" of teacher-estimated grades are costly, unreliable and rarely change anything.
- There is no statutory requirement for any internal tracking system, and none for a particular format.

## What the evidence says

**'Making data work' (2018).** The group found that data collection had become a major driver of workload, much of it producing information too unreliable to support the inferences drawn from it. Its principles: be clear about the purpose of any data; make collection efficient; and be confident the data is valid for that use. Specific messages, as summarised from the report (exact wording not re-checked against the PDF for this entry):

- No more than two or three attainment data collection points per year.
- Do not ask teachers to predict or track attainment in ways that imply false precision, such as sub-grades or "flight paths" from Key Stage 2.
- Pupil targets should not be set mechanically from prior attainment, nor used in teacher appraisal as a sole measure.
- Leaders, governors, trusts and local authorities should not request data in extra formats or at extra times.
- Ofsted should not ask for internal data in a particular form; inspection handbooks from 2019 said inspectors would not look at schools' internal progress and attainment data. Not verified: how the framework in force since November 2025 words this.

**Commission on Assessment Without Levels (2015).** Distinguished three purposes: in-school formative assessment, in-school summative assessment and nationally standardised summative assessment. A single assessment rarely serves all three well. It warned against recreating levels and against recording formative judgements in tracking systems.

**Measurement basics.** Any test samples a domain and carries error; small changes between collection points are usually noise. Teacher-assessed grades on short topic tests cannot reliably be mapped to GCSE grades, which describe performance across a full specification under exam conditions.

## In the classroom

- **Design cumulative tests.** An end-of-term Year 8 science test should sample everything taught so far this year, not just the last unit, so the score says something about retained learning.
- **Report raw scores or percentages** with the class or year average for context, rather than converting a 30-mark history test into a GCSE grade.
- **Use common assessments.** All classes in the year sit the same paper under similar conditions, marked to an agreed scheme and moderated, so results can be compared.
- **Keep formative information out of the tracker.** Quiz scores, hinge question responses and homework marks stay in the teacher's markbook to inform teaching.
- **Act on the results.** After the Year 10 mock, the department identifies the three weakest topics by question and adjusts the scheme of work; the class teacher identifies pupils for specific reteaching.

## For leaders

- Map the assessment calendar for a single pupil and a single teacher. Count the hours of marking and data entry, then ask what decisions each collection informed last year.
- A defensible secondary model: Key Stage 3, two summative points (one mid-year, one end-of-year exam) in each subject; Key Stage 4 and 5, mocks plus one other common assessment per year. Scale down for subjects taught once a week.
- Align collection points with reports to parents and with intervention planning so one collection serves several uses.
- Stop collecting: predicted grades every half-term; "working at" grades in Year 7; effort grades on multiple scales; progress RAG ratings derived from sub-grades.
- Present data to governors with its uncertainty: cohort-level patterns, standardised test results and external exam results are more trustworthy than internal grade forecasts.
- Trusts should take common data once and not ask for re-keying. Check the MIS can export it.

## Cautions and critiques

- **Over-frequent data drops.** The main critique, from Allen and from the workload group, is that six collections a year of estimated grades give an illusion of control. Changes between drops are mostly measurement error, and teachers learn to enter numbers that keep leaders satisfied.
- **Predictions are weak.** Teacher predictions of individual GCSE grades are often wrong by a grade or more; aggregate forecasts are somewhat better. Leaders should not treat the gap between prediction and outcome as a performance measure for teachers.
- **The opposite risk.** Too little summative assessment leaves pupils unpractised at extended exams and leaders without warning of problems. Two or three points a year is advice on collection, not a cap on testing in class.
- **Curriculum-linked versus standardised.** Internal tests show whether your curriculum was learned; they cannot show how pupils compare nationally. Standardised tests do the reverse. Know which question you are asking.
- **Accountability pressure remains.** Critics note that, whatever national advice says, trusts and governors anxious about results often still demand frequent forecasts.
