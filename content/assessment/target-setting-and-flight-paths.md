## At a glance

- A flight path plots an expected route, usually in GCSE grades or sub-grades, from a pupil's Key Stage 2 result to a Year 11 target, and labels the pupil above, on or below track at each data point.
- There is no statutory or Ofsted requirement to set individual target grades or to use flight paths.
- 'Making data work' (2018) advised against them: they assume linear progress, imply precision the data does not have, and generate workload.
- Estimates from FFT or similar are probability distributions for groups of similar pupils. Turning the most likely grade into a personal target misuses them.
- Better: high expectations for all, expressed through the curriculum; honest reporting of current performance on real assessments; cohort-level estimates for leaders.

## What the evidence says

**Progress is not linear.** Analysis by Education Datalab (2015) of national data found that only a small minority of pupils (around one in ten) follow the "expected" trajectory at every key stage between Key Stage 1 and Key Stage 4; most have uneven paths. Not verified: the figure is cited from memory of the Datalab report and should be checked before quoting.

**Estimates are distributions.** Models such as FFT Aspire estimate, for pupils with similar prior attainment and context, the percentage chance of each grade. A pupil whose most likely grade is 5 may have a substantial chance of a 4 or a 6 and a real chance of a 3 or 7. Key Stage 2 tests in reading and maths are weak predictors of outcomes in art, music, PE or languages five years later.

**Cohort gaps.** Pupils who should have sat Key Stage 2 tests in 2020 and 2021 have no SATs results, which is why Progress 8 could not be calculated for 2025 and 2026 GCSE cohorts. Schools relying on SATs-based flight paths had no baseline for those year groups; see the performance measures entry for the current position.

**Official advice.** The Commission on Assessment Without Levels (2015) warned schools against rebuilding levels under another name. The Teacher Workload Advisory Group (2018) criticised flight paths specifically and said targets should not be generated mechanically from prior attainment or used alone to judge teachers. DfE and Ofsted accepted its recommendations.

**Expectations and motivation.** Research on goal-setting suggests specific, challenging goals can help when people have the means to reach them; research on teacher expectations and on labelling suggests low targets can become self-fulfilling. Neither literature tests school target-grade systems directly. Evidence that giving pupils a target grade improves attainment is lacking.

## In the classroom

- **Talk about the work, not the number.** "To write a stronger evaluation you need a justified conclusion; here is one" is usable. "You are a 4b and need to be a 5c" is not.
- **Do not differentiate the curriculum by target grade.** Seating plans annotated with targets, "grade 4 task / grade 7 task" worksheets and tiered objectives lower the ceiling for pupils with low prior attainment.
- **Share real performance.** After a common assessment, give the score, the year-group average and the two topics to revisit.
- **In exam years,** it is reasonable to discuss a pupil's aspirations and what sixth-form or college courses require, alongside mock results. That is a conversation, not a tracked target.
- **Tiering and options decisions** (maths, science, MFL tiers) should rest on recent assessment evidence, not on a Year 7 flight path.

## For leaders

- If targets are kept, set them as aspirational ranges or minimum expectations for the cohort, be transparent that they are estimates, and never show pupils a target below a standard pass.
- Use FFT or trust benchmarks at cohort and subject level to ask "are our results in line with similar pupils nationally?" That is what the data is good for.
- Remove "progress towards target" RAG columns at Key Stage 3. Replace with attainment on common assessments and standardised reading and maths scores.
- Decouple pupil target grades from teacher appraisal. The 2018 report and subsequent DfE appraisal guidance discourage targets based on pupil outcome data alone.
- Explain the change to parents, who often value a single target grade. Reports can show current attainment, attitude and specific next steps instead.
- For sixth form, published transition matrices and value-added tools give minimum expected grades from GCSE; the same cautions apply, though prior attainment is more closely related to the subject.

## Cautions and critiques

- **False precision.** Sub-grades (5+, 5, 5−) and termly "expected progress" increments have no measurement basis.
- **Capping.** Critics including Tom Sherrington and Becky Allen argue that low targets tell pupils what the school thinks they are worth; disadvantaged pupils with low Key Stage 2 scores are most affected.
- **Demotivation at both ends.** Pupils far below an inflated target give up; pupils who have "met" a target coast.
- **Gaming.** Teachers under pressure enter "on track" data; leaders see a reassuring picture until results day.
- **Workload.** Maintaining flight paths across ten subjects and five year groups consumes hours of data entry for little decision value.
- **The counter-view.** Some leaders argue that without targets, low expectations go unchallenged, and that pupils and parents want a clear goal. A reasonable response is to hold high expectations through curriculum and assessment standards rather than through individual numerical forecasts.
