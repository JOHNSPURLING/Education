## At a glance

- Whole-class feedback (WCF) replaces individual written comments with a short teacher record of common errors, misconceptions and good examples, followed by reteaching and a pupil improvement task.
- Its main proven benefit is time: small school-based studies report marking time per book falling to a fraction of what individual comments take.
- There is no large trial showing it raises attainment more than written marking; equally, there is little robust evidence that written marking beats it.
- It works when the feedback lesson changes what pupils do. A slide of generic targets read aloud is not feedback.
- Some pupils, especially those with unusual errors or working well above or below the class, still need an individual word.

## What the evidence says

WCF grew out of English schools' response to the 2016 workload review and the EEF/Oxford finding that written marking is poorly evidenced. It is consistent with the EEF's 2021 guidance report, which says method matters less than whether feedback is built on good teaching, targets the task or subject, and is acted on by pupils.

Direct evidence is thin and mostly small-scale. Practitioner studies published by the Chartered College of Teaching report large reductions in marking time (one school reported roughly five minutes per book falling to about one and a half) with no apparent fall in homework completion or attainment in that context. A small Oxford study cited in the same literature found pupils *perceived* individual feedback as more useful and could not establish a causal effect on attainment. These are not randomised trials and should be treated as indicative only.

Daisy Christodoulou and others have argued that WCF's real advantage is not only workload: generic written comments ("add more analysis") are often less useful than a concrete reteach using real examples from the class, which is what a good WCF lesson provides. That is a reasoned argument rather than a trial finding.

## In the classroom

A workable routine for a set of Year 10 geography six-mark answers:

1. **Read, don't write.** Skim every answer (30 books in 20–30 minutes). Sort into three rough piles: secure, nearly there, not there.
2. **Record on one sheet.** Common misconceptions (for example, confusing weathering with erosion); missing elements (no named example); spelling of key terms; two or three strong sentences worth showing; names of pupils who need a conversation.
3. **Plan the response, not just the report.** Decide what to reteach, which model to show, and what task pupils will do.
4. **Feedback lesson (15–25 minutes).**
   - Show a strong anonymised answer under the visualiser and unpick why it works.
   - Reteach the main misconception with a fresh example and check understanding with a quick question.
   - Pupils correct listed spellings and then complete an improvement task: rewrite the weakest paragraph, or answer a parallel question.
5. **Circulate** to the pupils flagged for an individual check.
6. **Follow up.** Set a similar question two weeks later to see whether the error has gone.

Subject variations:

- **Maths:** WCF after a homework or quiz usually means identifying the two or three questions most pupils got wrong, modelling them again, and setting near-identical questions.
- **English:** show two contrasting paragraphs from the class, co-edit one live, then pupils redraft their own.
- **MFL:** list the five most frequent grammatical errors as sentences for pupils to correct before fixing their own work.
- **Art, DT, PE:** group critique of work or performance against criteria, with one specific refinement for the next attempt.

Avoid giving each pupil a code that simply maps to a generic target; pupils need to locate the issue in their own work, with help if necessary.

## For leaders

- Allow departments to adopt WCF where it suits the task, rather than mandating a whole-school proforma. A compulsory WCF sheet that must be completed, filed and stuck in books recreates the compliance culture it was meant to remove.
- Train staff in the feedback lesson, which is the hard part: selecting examples, reteaching, designing improvement tasks.
- Tell parents and pupils why books have fewer written comments and what happens instead.
- Quality-assure by watching feedback lessons and comparing before-and-after work, not by collecting sheets.
- Keep individual written feedback where it has clear value: extended essays, A-level work, and coursework within exam board rules on permitted feedback.

## Cautions and critiques

- **Not individualised.** Critics argue pupils with atypical errors, high attainers and pupils with SEND can be missed. Mitigation: the "names" box, live circulation and adapting the improvement task.
- **Pupils may not recognise the feedback as theirs.** Novices often cannot tell which of the listed errors apply to them; they need the teacher to point, or a checklist to self-audit against.
- **Can become a fad.** Christodoulou has asked whether WCF is "saviour or fad"; the risk is the format spreading while the reteaching disappears.
- **Evidence gap.** Claims that WCF is "more effective" than marking are not supported by trial evidence; the defensible claim is that it is far cheaper in time and plausibly no worse.
- **It is still reactive.** Frequent WCF on the same error suggests the original teaching or task design needs changing.
