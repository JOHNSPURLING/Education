## At a glance

- This is the DfE's main advice document on behaviour. It is **non-statutory**, but it describes legal duties that sit in Acts and regulations, and Ofsted and tribunals treat it as the benchmark of reasonable practice.
- The version in force is the one substantially rewritten in 2022 (in effect from September 2022) and last revised on 19 February 2024. As far as could be established on 12 November 2026 it has not been reissued since, although several documents it cross-refers to changed in 2026.
- Its central argument: good behaviour is created by a deliberately designed whole-school culture, taught explicitly, led visibly by the headteacher and applied consistently by all staff.
- It introduced or formalised three ideas now common in secondary schools: a **behaviour curriculum**, **removal from classrooms** as a serious sanction with safeguards, and the expectation that schools **anticipate** likely triggers for pupils with SEND.
- Read it alongside the 2026 exclusions guidance, the 2026 restrictive interventions guidance and the now-statutory mobile phones guidance: the 2024 text predates all three.

## Status and current version

- **Title:** Behaviour in schools: advice for headteachers and school staff.
- **Applies to:** maintained schools, academies and free schools, non-maintained special schools, pupil referral units and, as the February 2024 revision made explicit, independent schools.
- **Version history:** it replaced 'Behaviour and discipline in schools' (2016) after a consultation in early 2022. The February 2024 revision was narrow: it rewrote the mobile phones section to match the separate phones guidance published the same day.
- **What has moved around it since:** the mobile phones guidance became statutory in 2026; the 2013 reasonable force advice was replaced from 1 April 2026; new exclusions guidance applies from 26 July 2026. Where the 2024 behaviour guidance summarises those areas, the newer document prevails.
- Not verified: whether DfE made any minor edit to this document after February 2024. GOV.UK could not be opened directly when this entry was written; the dating above rests on search results and on 2026 legal commentary that still describes it as the February 2024 guidance.

## What schools must do

The 'musts' come from legislation, not from the guidance itself.

- **Maintained schools:** the headteacher must determine measures to promote good behaviour, self-discipline and respect, and to prevent bullying, and must publicise them in a written behaviour policy (Education and Inspections Act 2006, s.89). The governing body must make and review a written statement of behaviour principles (s.88). The policy must be brought to the attention of pupils, parents and staff at least once a year and published on the school website.
- **Academies and independent schools:** the proprietor must ensure a written policy to promote good behaviour is drawn up and implemented effectively, with sanctions set out (Independent School Standards Regulations 2014).
- **Sanctions must be lawful:** imposed by a paid member of staff or someone authorised by the head, on the premises or while the pupil is under staff charge, and reasonable and proportionate in all the circumstances, taking account of age, SEND and religious requirements (s.91).
- **Equality and SEND law applies throughout:** no discrimination, reasonable adjustments for disabled pupils, and best endeavours to meet special educational needs.
- **Safeguarding:** staff must consider whether misbehaviour gives cause to suspect a pupil is suffering, or likely to suffer, harm.

## In practice

What the guidance says schools **should** do:

- **Define the culture.** State the behaviour you want in concrete terms, not just what is banned. The head and senior team should be visible and should model it.
- **Teach a behaviour curriculum.** Routines and expectations are taught, practised and re-taught, with induction for new pupils and mid-year arrivals. Adjust routines for individual pupils where needed rather than lowering the expectation.
- **Train staff.** All staff, including support and site staff, should know the policy and their role in it, with induction and regular refreshers.
- **Respond predictably.** Every incident gets a response that is consistent, fair and proportionate. Sanctions have stated purposes: deterrence, protection, improvement.
- **Follow up.** After a sanction, consider a conversation with the pupil, contact with parents, or a targeted intervention, so the same behaviour is less likely next time.
- **Removal from classrooms** should be for serious disruption, for a limited time, in a supervised place where education continues, governed by a published policy, with parents informed the same day and data monitored by group. It is distinct from a pupil choosing to use a separation or regulation space.
- **Behaviour outside school and online.** The policy can cover conduct off site when the pupil is identifiable as a member of the school, travelling to or from it, or where the behaviour could affect the school's orderly running or threaten others.
- **Child-on-child sexual violence and harassment** should never be passed off as banter; respond in line with Keeping Children Safe in Education.
- **Use data.** Leaders should analyse behaviour incidents, removals, suspensions and attendance together, by pupil characteristic, to spot patterns and test whether the policy works.

A secondary example: a head of year sees one Year 9 pupil removed from science six times in three weeks. The guidance points towards asking what precedes the removals (seating, literacy demand, a particular lesson slot), involving the SENCO, agreeing an adjustment and a re-entry routine, and reviewing in two weeks, while the removal sanction itself still stands.

## Common pitfalls

- **Treating it as law, or as optional.** It is neither. Departing from it needs a reason you could defend to governors, Ofsted or a tribunal.
- **A policy that describes sanctions but not teaching.** The guidance expects the positive curriculum and the staff training to be as detailed as the consequences.
- **'Consistency' used to refuse adjustments.** The guidance is explicit that schools should anticipate SEND-related triggers. Blanket application that disadvantages a disabled pupil can be unlawful discrimination.
- **Removal drifting into routine.** Long or repeated stays, no curriculum work, no record and no parental contact are the features the guidance warns against.
- **Reading the 2024 text on phones, force or exclusion in isolation.** Each has been superseded by a 2026 document.
