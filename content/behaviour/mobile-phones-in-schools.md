## At a glance

- The DfE expects every school to have a policy under which pupils **do not have access to their phones during the school day**: lessons, movement between lessons, break and lunch.
- The guidance became **statutory on 29 June 2026** under the Children's Wellbeing and Schools Act 2026. Schools are expected to follow it from **1 September 2026**.
- Statutory guidance means schools must **have regard** to it and need a good, recorded reason to depart from it. Ministers have described this as stopping short of an outright legislative ban, but 'phones out of sight in bags' policies that tolerate use are no longer in line with the expectation.
- Schools still choose **how**: no phones on site, hand-in on arrival, lockers, or locked pouches.
- Exceptions for medical need, disability and certain individual circumstances (for example young carers) remain and are a legal matter under the Equality Act 2010.
- Ofsted is reported to consider how well the policy is implemented and understood from the 2026 to 2027 year.

## Status and current version

- **History:** non-statutory guidance first published 19 February 2024; substantially revised 19 January 2026, tightening the expectation to a fully phone-free day; made statutory on 29 June 2026 following Royal Assent to the Act on 29 April 2026.
- **Applies to:** state-funded schools in England have the 'have regard' duty. Not verified: whether and how the statutory duty extends to independent schools and to 16 to 19 academies; secondary summaries conflict. Check the guidance's scope section.
- **Sixth forms:** not verified. Earlier versions allowed schools discretion over sixth-form pupils' use while expecting them not to undermine the policy for younger pupils; confirm against the current text.
- **Other points reported from the 2026 version:** boarding and residential schools should prohibit phones during the teaching day; 'bring your own device' schemes should not rely on phones; staff should not use personal phones in front of pupils other than for work reasons.
- GOV.UK could not be opened directly when this entry was written; the dates are taken from the GOV.UK page text returned in search results and from law firm commentary.

## What schools must do

- **Have regard to the guidance** when setting and applying the behaviour policy.
- **Set out the phone rules in the behaviour policy** (or a linked policy), including sanctions and how confiscated phones are kept and returned, and publicise it to pupils, parents and staff.
- **Make reasonable adjustments** for disabled pupils, for example a pupil who uses a phone app to monitor glucose, and support pupils with medical conditions (Children and Families Act 2014, s.100).
- **Confiscate lawfully.** Confiscation is a permitted sanction if the policy says so; the law protects staff from liability for loss or damage where confiscation is lawful. Searches for phones must follow the searching guidance: a phone is only searchable without consent if the school rules list it as an item that may be searched for.
- **Treat safeguarding content correctly.** Do not view or delete suspected indecent images; pass to the DSL.

## In practice

Four models, with trade-offs seen in secondary schools:

| Model | Works when | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Not on premises | Small sites, short journeys | Parental worry about travel; little way to check |
| Hand in on arrival | Smaller rolls, staffed reception | Queues, liability, storage |
| Lockers or locked pouches | Larger schools with budget | Cost, damaged pouches, decoy phones |
| 'Never used, seen or heard' (in bag, off) | Strong existing culture | Hardest to enforce; toilets and social times; may not meet the 2026 expectation if use persists |

Implementation steps:

1. **Explain why** to pupils and parents before launch: focus in lessons, fewer online conflicts spilling into school, more talk at lunch. Give the office number for urgent messages.
2. **One script for all staff.** "Phone, please. You know the rule. It'll be at reception at 3.10." No debate, no first warnings that vary by teacher.
3. **Fixed consequence ladder,** for example: first time, confiscated to end of day; second, parent collects; third, hand in daily for two weeks. Keep it short and publish it.
4. **Record** each confiscation in the behaviour system so repeat patterns and hotspots show.
5. **Log exceptions** individually, with a named member of staff, and brief teachers discreetly.
6. **Cover the gaps:** toilets, the route out at the end of the day, smartwatches, earbuds, and second 'decoy' handsets.
7. **Replace what phones did:** bus timetables, payment for lunch, homework apps, timetable access.

## Common pitfalls

- Staff applying the rule differently; pupils quickly learn which lessons are safe.
- Sanctions that keep a phone overnight without parental contact, raising safety concerns on the journey home.
- Refusing individual adjustments on 'consistency' grounds.
- Overclaiming the evidence. The University of Birmingham SMART Schools study (2025) of 30 English secondaries found restrictive policies cut in-school phone use but found no difference in wellbeing, attainment or behaviour measures between restrictive and permissive schools; it was cross-sectional, so cannot show cause. Other studies, including earlier LSE work on attainment, point the other way. Honest messaging: a phone-free day is a behaviour and attention policy, not a mental health cure.
- Forgetting staff modelling.
