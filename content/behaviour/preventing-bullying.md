## At a glance

- Every school must have measures to prevent **all forms of bullying**. For maintained schools these sit in the behaviour policy by law; academies and independent schools must have an effective anti-bullying strategy.
- The DfE's working definition: behaviour by an individual or group, **repeated over time**, that **intentionally hurts** another individual or group physically or emotionally, often involving an **imbalance of power**.
- The DfE advice is short and non-prescriptive. The version dated **July 2017** is still the one schools cite in 2026 policies; no replacement for pupil bullying was found.
- A bullying incident becomes a **child protection** concern where there is reasonable cause to suspect significant harm. Some bullying is also a crime.
- Separate non-statutory DfE guidance on bullying and harassment of **school staff** was published in July 2026.

## What schools must do

- **Maintained schools:** the headteacher must determine measures to prevent all forms of bullying among pupils, as part of the behaviour policy, and communicate them to pupils, staff and parents (Education and Inspections Act 2006, s.89).
- **Academies and independent schools:** the proprietor must ensure bullying is prevented in so far as reasonably practicable by drawing up and implementing an effective anti-bullying strategy (Independent School Standards).
- **Equality Act 2010:** schools must not discriminate against, harass or victimise pupils, and the public sector equality duty requires due regard to eliminating harassment and fostering good relations across protected characteristics.
- **Children Act 1989:** treat bullying as a safeguarding matter where a child is suffering, or likely to suffer, significant harm.
- **Outside school:** headteachers have the power, not a duty, to discipline for conduct off the premises to a reasonable extent, which covers journeys and online behaviour.

There is no legal requirement for a separate anti-bullying policy, though many schools have one. There is no statutory duty to record bullying incidents, but the DfE advises schools to develop their own approach and Ofsted will ask to see records and analysis.

## In practice

**Prevention**

- Teach what bullying is and is not (one-off fallings-out, mutual conflict) in tutor time and RSHE, using the three tests: repeated, intended, power imbalance.
- Name prejudice-based bullying explicitly: racist, homophobic, biphobic, transphobic, sexist, disablist, faith-based, and about appearance or home circumstances.
- Supervise the places it happens: toilets, changing rooms, buses, the dinner queue, group chats.
- Staff challenge 'banter' every time. Script: "That's not a joke here. We don't use that word. See me at the end."

**Reporting**

- Several routes: tutor, head of year, an online form or QR code, a trusted-adult list. Pupils often will not report face to face.
- Tell pupils what will happen next and who will know.

**Responding**

1. Listen to the targeted pupil first; ask what they want to happen and what they fear.
2. Take separate accounts from those involved and from bystanders. Keep screenshots; do not forward imagery.
3. Decide: is this bullying, and is there a safeguarding or criminal element? Involve the DSL where needed.
4. Apply the sanction in the behaviour policy and tell the targeted pupil's parents what was done in general terms.
5. Work with the pupil who bullied on why, and on what changes.
6. Check in with the targeted pupil at one, two and six weeks. Most failure is here: the behaviour resumes more quietly.

Restorative meetings can help in some cases, but only with the targeted pupil's free agreement; putting a victim opposite a more powerful peer can entrench the harm.

**For leaders**

- Record incidents by type and by protected characteristic; report termly to governors.
- Survey pupils annually; compare with the National Behaviour Survey, which tracks pupil-reported bullying nationally.
- Train all staff, including lunchtime and transport staff.

## Common pitfalls

- A threshold so high that nothing is ever logged as bullying.
- Asking the targeted pupil to change behaviour, seat or route while the perpetrator's routine stays the same.
- Treating online bullying as 'happened outside school'.
- Recording the first incident but not the follow-up.
- Assuming bought-in programmes work automatically. Whole-school anti-bullying programmes show modest average reductions; the 2024 UK trial of KiVa in 118 primary schools reported about a 13 per cent reduction, smaller than in Finland. Evidence in secondary schools is thinner.
