## At a glance

- Child-on-child abuse includes bullying (including online and prejudice-based), abuse in teenage relationships, physical abuse, sexual violence, sexual harassment, upskirting, sharing of nudes, and initiation or hazing rituals.
- KCSIE's starting point: assume it **is happening** in your school even if nothing is reported, and never dismiss it as banter or part of growing up.
- Part five of KCSIE 2026 gives the detailed process for sexual harassment and sexual violence. It was restructured in 2026 around a continuum of harmful sexual behaviour, with harassment addressed first.
- Every report needs: reassurance for the child who reports, a recorded risk and needs assessment where appropriate, a decision on the response route, and support for all children involved.
- Upskirting and sexual offences are crimes; rape, assault by penetration and sexual assault reports should be passed to the police, normally alongside a social care referral.

## Status and current version

Statutory guidance: KCSIE 2026, Part five and the child-on-child abuse content in Parts one and two, in force 1 September 2026. The separate DfE advice 'Sexual violence and sexual harassment between children in schools and colleges' was withdrawn and merged into KCSIE in 2022.

**What changed in 2026** (from legal and NSPCC briefings; confirm against the text):

- Part five reorganised to reflect a continuum from harmful sexual behaviour through harassment to sexual violence, rather than leading with sexual violence.
- **Misogyny** named more explicitly and more often as a safeguarding concern.
- A new point that **all** incidents of sharing nudes or semi-nudes need a safeguarding response, whether or not the sharing was consensual.
- Referral language updated to Family Help, in line with 'Working together' 2026.
- Terminology aligned with 'Behaviour in schools' (for example 'sanction').
- Not verified: the exact wording of the revised Part five heading and paragraph numbers.

Context remains Ofsted's 2021 review of sexual abuse in schools, which found harassment and online sexual abuse so common that many pupils saw no point in reporting.

## What schools must do

Schools **must** have regard to KCSIE and comply with the Equality Act 2010, including the public sector equality duty, and the Human Rights Act 1998. KCSIE says the child protection policy **should** include:

- procedures to minimise the risk of child-on-child abuse;
- how allegations are recorded, investigated and dealt with;
- clear processes for supporting victims, alleged perpetrators and other children affected;
- a statement that abuse is never tolerated or passed off as banter;
- recognition that it may be happening without reports, and of the gendered nature of much sexual harassment while taking all reports seriously.

The DSL **should** consider a risk and needs assessment for every report of sexual violence, and case by case for harassment, covering the victim, the alleged perpetrator and other pupils, including time and location and transport. For rape or assault by penetration, the alleged perpetrator should be removed from shared classes while arrangements are considered.

## In practice

**Response options** in Part five: manage internally; early help or Family Help; referral to children's social care; report to the police. Record the decision and the reasons each time. A police decision to take no further action, or a not-guilty verdict, does not end the school's duty to support both children and manage risk.

**Staff:**

- challenge sexualised language, comments on appearance, and unwanted touching every time; record it so patterns are visible;
- take a report without leading questions, with a second adult where possible, and do not view or forward images;
- tell the child what happens next and pass to the DSL the same day.

**DSLs:**

- hold a written risk assessment and review it as circumstances change;
- keep the victim's wishes central, including about how any separation works, while being clear when a referral must be made regardless;
- treat the child alleged to have caused harm as a child with possible unmet needs or own experience of abuse;
- log incidents by type, year group, place and time; use pupil voice surveys to find the unreported.

**Governors and trustees:** ask for anonymised trend data, pupil survey results, how RSHE is addressing consent, pornography and misogyny, and how the behaviour policy and child protection policy fit together.

## Common pitfalls

- Low incident numbers taken as assurance rather than as a reporting-culture question.
- Waiting for the police outcome before acting on risk or support.
- A sanction issued but no risk assessment or safeguarding analysis; or the reverse.
- Victim moved or timetabled away as the default, which punishes the reporter.
- Online harm between pupils out of school hours treated as outside the school's remit.
- Disability, SEND and LGBT-related vulnerability overlooked in risk assessment.
- Treating a young person's malicious or unsubstantiated report without considering whether they need help.
