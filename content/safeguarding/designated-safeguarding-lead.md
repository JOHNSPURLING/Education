## At a glance

- KCSIE says every school **should** appoint an appropriate **senior member of staff from the leadership team** as DSL, with the role written into their job description.
- The DSL holds **lead responsibility** for safeguarding and child protection, including online safety and understanding filtering and monitoring. Tasks can be delegated to trained deputies; the lead responsibility cannot.
- KCSIE 2026 strengthens expectations about the DSL's skills and experience, deputy training, and **cover arrangements** when the DSL is unavailable.
- The DSL and deputies should have training that is updated at least every two years, with knowledge refreshed at least annually.
- The role needs time, funding, training, supervision and the authority to challenge. Under-resourcing the role is a governance failure, not a personal one.

## What the evidence says

The role is defined by statutory guidance rather than research. KCSIE 2026 has an annex setting out the role (the annex letter changed in 2026; check the current text). Its main headings are stable:

- **Availability:** the DSL or a deputy should always be available during school hours for staff to discuss concerns, in person where possible; adequate cover is needed for out-of-hours and out-of-term activities.
- **Managing referrals:** to children's social care, to the Channel programme for radicalisation concerns, to the Disclosure and Barring Service after a dismissal or resignation over risk to children, and to the police where a crime may have been committed.
- **Working with others:** point of contact for the safeguarding partners; liaison with the headteacher, the case manager and local authority designated officer for allegations, the SENCO, mental health lead, and IT and network staff.
- **Information and records:** keeping detailed, secure, separate child protection files and transferring them when a child moves (within five days for an in-year transfer or within the first five days of a new term).
- **Raising awareness:** the policy is known, reviewed at least annually and publicly available.
- **Training, knowledge and skills** and **understanding the views of children**.
- **Holding and sharing information** and helping promote the educational outcomes of children with a social worker.

Serious case and local child safeguarding practice reviews repeatedly identify the same weaknesses: information held but not joined up, concerns not escalated, and limited professional curiosity. Not verified: a specific national review citing school DSL capacity.

2026 changes reported by legal and training briefings: cover arrangements should be robust (trained deputies, shared safeguarding mailboxes, clear escalation); deputy DSL training expectations are aligned more closely with those for the DSL. Confirm wording in the published text.

## In the classroom

For teachers and support staff, the DSL system works only if they do their part:

- Know the DSL and deputies by name and photo, and how to reach them during lessons, trips and after-school activities.
- Report the same day, in the school's recording system, using the child's own words. Do not investigate, and do not wait to be sure.
- If you have reported and heard nothing, ask. If you still think a child is at risk, press the concern; KCSIE is explicit that any member of staff can make a referral.
- Concerns about an adult go to the headteacher (or chair of governors if about the head), not to the DSL in the first instance unless the policy says so.

## For leaders

**Capacity.** In a secondary school the role is rarely compatible with a heavy teaching load. Decide explicitly how many hours it needs, and resource a team: deputies by year group or key stage, an administrator for records and meeting logistics, and named leads for attendance, online safety and children in care who work with the DSL.

**Routines that work:**

- A daily triage of new concerns with decisions and rationale recorded.
- A weekly safeguarding team meeting reviewing open cases, attendance, behaviour and alternative provision data together.
- Half-termly case sampling by the head or a trust safeguarding lead.
- Reflective supervision for the DSL and deputies. KCSIE says they should be given support; formal supervision is good practice rather than a stated requirement.

**Governors and trustees should check:**

- the DSL is on the leadership team and the role is in the job description;
- cover is real: who is available on a residential, at a Saturday fixture, in August results week?
- training dates for the DSL and all deputies;
- the DSL's termly report covers trends, referral outcomes, escalations and the DSL's own capacity;
- the link safeguarding governor meets the DSL and tests systems, without seeing identifiable casework.

## Cautions and critiques

- 'Lead responsibility' is sometimes read as sole responsibility. Safeguarding is everyone's job; the head and governing body remain accountable.
- A DSL who is also behaviour lead, attendance lead and SENCO is a single point of failure.
- Common audit findings: no trained deputy on site during clubs or trips; child protection files not transferred or receipt not confirmed; decisions not to refer left unrecorded; DSL unaware of how the filtering and monitoring system works or who reviews alerts.
- The emotional load of the role is real; lack of supervision is a recognised risk for retention and decision quality.
