## At a glance

- Section 26 of the Counter-Terrorism and Security Act 2015 places a duty on schools to have **due regard to the need to prevent people from becoming terrorists or supporting terrorism**.
- The current statutory guidance is the Home Office's 'Prevent duty guidance: for England and Wales', published in September 2023 and in force from **31 December 2023**. No newer edition was found in November 2026.
- Prevent is treated as part of safeguarding: concerns go to the DSL and are handled like any other safeguarding concern, with the Channel programme as the specialist multi-agency route.
- The guidance uses 'susceptible to radicalisation' rather than 'vulnerable', and names the main threats as Islamist and extreme right-wing terrorism, while covering all ideologies and unclear or mixed motivations.
- Schools need a risk assessment, trained staff, IT policies that restrict access to terrorist material, and a curriculum that builds resilience without shutting down debate.

## Status and current version

**Who it applies to.** 'Specified authorities', which include maintained schools, academies, independent schools, alternative provision, sixth-form and FE colleges and registered childcare.

**Version.** The 2023 guidance replaced the 2015 guidance and brought all education settings into one document. Unions and the DfE were clear that it created no new legal requirements for schools. Changes included: the three themes for education (leadership and partnership; capabilities; reducing permissive environments); 'susceptibility' language; emphasis on ideology as a driver; and Prevent's first objective reworded to tackling the ideological causes of terrorism. Not verified: one school policy refers to a March 2025 amendment to the guidance; check the GOV.UK page for the update history.

KCSIE 2026 continues to direct schools to the Prevent duty guidance and summarises Prevent and Channel in its further-information annex.

Definitions: the guidance relies on the statutory definition of terrorism (Terrorism Act 2000) and on the government's definition of extremism, which was revised in March 2024 for use by government; that definition is not statutory.

## What schools must do

**Must:** have due regard to the duty in exercising their functions, and have regard to the statutory guidance in doing so.

The guidance says schools **should**:

- **Leadership and partnership:** leaders understand the local risk; a named lead (usually the DSL) oversees Prevent; the school works with the local authority Prevent lead, police and the safeguarding partnership; Prevent is built into existing safeguarding policies rather than a stand-alone bureaucracy.
- **Risk assessment:** assess the risk of pupils being radicalised, informed by local risk information; review at least annually; turn it into actions.
- **Capabilities:** train staff to recognise signs and know what to do; the DSL and safeguarding leads receive more in-depth training, refreshed at least every two years in line with the guidance.
- **Reducing permissive environments:** filtering to restrict access to terrorist and extremist material; policies on visiting speakers and lettings so the school is not used as a platform for radicalisers; build resilience through the curriculum, including fundamental British values.
- **Information sharing** in line with data protection law when making referrals.

## In practice

**Staff:**

- notice changes: fixation on an ideology or grievance, us-and-them language, new online contacts, justification of violence, symbols or content shared;
- 'notice, check, share': talk to the pupil if appropriate, check with colleagues who know them, share with the DSL the same day;
- keep classrooms open to discussion of controversial issues. Prevent does not require staff to report opinions or stop debate; schools remain under duties of political impartiality.

**DSLs:**

- consider the concern like any other: what is the need, what is the risk? Options include support in school, early help, or a **Prevent referral** using the national referral form via the local route (police or local authority, as local procedures specify);
- Channel is voluntary and requires consent (from parents for children); it is support, not a criminal sanction;
- keep the risk assessment live: local profile, online risk, pupils with unsupervised internet use, site lettings, visiting speakers;
- use the government's free Prevent duty training (awareness, referrals and Channel courses on GOV.UK) and the DfE regional Prevent co-ordinators.

**Governors and trustees:** check there is a current risk assessment and action plan, a visiting speaker and lettings procedure, staff training records, and that filtering tests include extremist content.

## Common pitfalls

- A generic downloaded risk assessment with no local content.
- Training done once, years ago, with no record for newer staff.
- Treating Prevent as separate from safeguarding, so wider needs (isolation, SEND, mental health, exploitation) are missed.
- Over-referral based on a pupil's faith or politics rather than behaviour and susceptibility; this damages trust and has been a long-standing criticism of Prevent from some community groups, unions and academics. The 2023 independent review argued the opposite risk, that the programme had drifted from tackling ideology. Schools should be aware both critiques exist.
- No vetting of external speakers or hirers.
- Ignoring online spaces, gaming platforms and mixed or unclear ideologies, including fascination with mass violence.
