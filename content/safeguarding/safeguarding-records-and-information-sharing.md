## At a glance

- Record every concern, discussion, decision and the reason for it, in writing, on the day. KCSIE says this **should** happen; reviews of serious cases show why.
- Data protection law does **not** prevent sharing information to keep a child safe. Fear of sharing must not stand in the way of safeguarding.
- **New from 30 September 2026:** a statutory **information sharing duty** (section 16LA of the Children Act 2004, inserted by the Children's Wellbeing and Schools Act 2026) requires specified agencies to share information relevant to safeguarding or promoting a child's welfare. Schools and colleges are reported to be in scope.
- New DfE statutory guidance on information sharing was published in September 2026 to accompany the duty. The 2024 non-statutory 'Information sharing: advice for practitioners' and its 'seven golden rules' remain a useful practical summary. Not verified: whether the 2024 advice has been withdrawn or updated; check GOV.UK.
- When a pupil moves, the child protection file follows them, separately from the main file, within five days.

## What the evidence says

**Recording (KCSIE 2026).** Records should include a clear and comprehensive summary of the concern; details of how it was followed up and resolved; and a note of any action taken, decisions reached and the outcome. Files are kept confidential and secure, separate from the pupil file, and seen only by those who need to.

**Transfer.** The DSL should transfer the child protection file to the new school or college as soon as possible, within five days for an in-year move or within the first five days of the new term, by secure means with confirmation of receipt. The DSL should also consider sharing key information in advance so support is in place on arrival.

**The information sharing duty (from 30 September 2026).** As described in the DfE consultation and by legal and partnership briefings:

- it applies between 'relevant persons' listed in the legislation, and covers information about the child and about other people connected to them where relevant to the child's welfare;
- where the duty applies, consent is not the legal basis and is not required, though being open with families remains good practice unless it would increase risk;
- absence of a data sharing agreement should not delay sharing;
- it does not apply where sharing would be more detrimental to the child than not sharing;
- it ends when the young person turns 18;
- bodies under the duty must have regard to the statutory guidance.

Not verified: the precise list of education bodies designated in regulations, and the final title of the statutory guidance. Read the GOV.UK document and your local partnership's briefing.

**Single unique identifier.** The same Act provides for a consistent identifier for children across services (the NHS number has been piloted). Not verified: that this provision has been commenced or that schools have any current action to take.

**Data protection.** UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018 permit sharing for safeguarding. Schools usually rely on 'public task' or 'legal obligation' as the lawful basis, and on the 'safeguarding of children and individuals at risk' condition for special category data. Schools may withhold data from a subject access request where the serious harm test is met.

**Other practitioner guidance.** 'What to do if you're worried a child is being abused: advice for practitioners' (DfE, 2015) remains the short non-statutory guide to recognising and reporting abuse. Not verified: any later revision.

## In the classroom

What every member of staff does:

- Write it down the same day in the school's system: date, time, place, who was present, what you saw or heard, the child's exact words in quotation marks, what you said, and what you did.
- Fact first, opinion labelled as opinion. Use a body map for injuries; do not photograph injuries.
- Record 'small' things: hunger, changes in presentation, a remark. Patterns are only visible if someone logs the pieces.
- Do not keep private notes, and do not share on messaging apps or personal email.
- Tell the child you will need to pass it on; never promise secrecy.

## For leaders

**DSLs:**

- keep a running **chronology** at the front of each file, including attendance, behaviour and health events, and review it before every decision;
- record decisions **not** to refer or share, with reasons;
- before sharing, ask: is it necessary, proportionate, relevant, accurate, timely and secure? Record what was shared, with whom and why;
- check that the system's access permissions match roles, and that leavers lose access;
- set retention in the records policy (commonly until the child's 25th birthday; longer for some categories). Since the Independent Inquiry into Child Sexual Abuse, many schools retain records relating to sexual abuse for much longer; take advice from your data protection officer;
- update privacy notices and the child protection policy to reflect the new duty.

**Governors and trustees** check: an annual audit of file quality; evidence that files were sent and received for leavers and requested for joiners; a data protection impact assessment for the recording system; staff training on the information sharing duty.

## Cautions and critiques

- Common audit failings: undated entries, no outcome recorded, concerns logged under behaviour rather than safeguarding, files not transferred at post-16 transition, and siblings' records not linked.
- Over-reliance on consent has historically delayed sharing; the new duty is designed to change this. Some home education and privacy groups have criticised the duty's breadth; schools should still be transparent with families.
- Electronic systems make logging easy and analysis no easier: someone must read the chronology.
