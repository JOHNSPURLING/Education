## At a glance

- Working memory can hold and process only a few new items at once; long-term memory is effectively unlimited. Learning means building knowledge (schemas) in long-term memory.
- Once knowledge is secure it no longer taxes working memory, which is why experts cope with complexity that swamps novices.
- Teachers can manage load by cutting what is irrelevant (extraneous load) and by sequencing and breaking down what is inherently hard (intrinsic load).
- The best-supported classroom implications are worked examples for novices, integrating text with diagrams, removing redundant material and fading guidance as expertise grows.
- 'Reduce cognitive load' does not mean 'make it easy'. The aim is to spend limited attention on the thing to be learned.

## What the evidence says

Sweller set out the theory in 1988 from experiments showing that novices who solved problems by search learned less than those who studied solutions. The theory has since generated a family of 'effects', mostly from controlled experiments:

- **Worked example effect.** Novices learn more from studying worked solutions than from solving equivalent problems.
- **Split-attention effect.** Learning suffers when pupils must mentally combine separated sources, such as a diagram and a key on another page. Integrate labels into the diagram.
- **Redundancy effect.** Presenting the same information twice (reading slides aloud word for word, decorative extras) can impair learning.
- **Modality effect.** A diagram with spoken explanation can work better than a diagram with dense written text.
- **Expertise reversal effect.** Supports that help novices become unnecessary, and sometimes harmful, for more knowledgeable learners.
- **Guidance fading.** Move from full worked examples to partially completed ones to independent problems.

The theory distinguishes **intrinsic load** (the complexity of the material relative to what the learner already knows) from **extraneous load** (caused by poor presentation or task design). A third type, 'germane' load, appears in older accounts; Sweller and colleagues have since played it down, treating it as the working memory devoted to intrinsic load.

The New South Wales CESE review (2017) is the most-used teacher summary and notes that the theory rests on a large number of randomised experiments. The EEF's 2021 review of cognitive science in classrooms found support for worked examples (chiefly secondary maths and science) and for scaffolding to manage load in Key Stages 2–4 maths, science and reading comprehension, while stressing that classroom-based evidence is thin for other subjects and ages.

## In the classroom

- **Explanations.** One new idea at a time. In Year 7 maths, teach collecting like terms with positive coefficients before introducing negatives.
- **Slides and resources.** Remove clip art, animations and paragraphs you intend to say anyway. Put labels on the diagram of the heart, not in a numbered key. Hand out the diagram so pupils do not copy and listen simultaneously.
- **Talk and text.** Do not talk over pupils while they read or write. Give the instruction, then silence.
- **Worked examples.** Pair each worked example with a near-identical problem ('example–problem pairs'), then fade: complete the last step, then the last two, then the whole.
- **Pre-teach components.** Secure vocabulary and number facts before the complex task, so they are not competing for attention during it. In a Year 10 chemistry required practical, teach the method and the apparatus names the lesson before.
- **Routines.** Familiar lesson routines free attention for content.
- **Adjust for expertise.** A Year 13 class revising a familiar topic needs problems, not more worked examples.

## For leaders

- Cognitive load theory is a useful **lens for reviewing resources and explanations** in department time: 'what in this booklet is not helping pupils learn the thing?'
- It should not be reduced to rules such as 'no more than X words on a slide' or bans on group work or images.
- The DfE's framework for trainee and early career teachers includes working memory and prior knowledge under 'how pupils learn', so new staff will arrive with the vocabulary. Check that it is being applied in subject-specific ways.
- Cost is negligible; the investment is collaborative planning time.

## Cautions and critiques

- **Measurement and falsifiability.** Cognitive load is usually inferred from self-ratings or from outcomes. Ton de Jong (2010) and others argue that, because load types cannot be measured separately, almost any result can be explained after the fact.
- **Lab-to-classroom gap.** Many experiments are short, with university students or with maths and science content. The EEF review cautions against assuming the effects transfer to every subject, age and timescale.
- **Motivation and emotion** are largely outside the theory. A sequence that is optimal for load can still be dull, and pupils who are anxious have less working memory to spare.
- **Tension with desirable difficulties.** The Bjorks' work shows that some difficulty during practice improves long-term retention. The usual reconciliation is: reduce load when first acquiring complex material, add difficulty when practising it.
- **Productive failure.** Kapur's studies suggest attempting problems before instruction can help conceptual understanding in some cases, which challenges a strict 'examples first' rule.
- **Overreach.** Didau argues teachers need the core idea (novices should not be left to problem-solve unfamiliar, complex content) rather than the full technical apparatus.
