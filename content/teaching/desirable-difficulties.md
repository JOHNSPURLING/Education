## At a glance

- **Performance** (what pupils can do during or just after a lesson) is an unreliable guide to **learning** (durable change in what they know and can do later).
- Some conditions depress performance now but strengthen learning: spacing, interleaving, retrieval instead of re-reading, generating an answer before being told, and varying the conditions of practice.
- A difficulty is 'desirable' only if pupils have the knowledge to overcome it. Otherwise it is just difficult.
- The idea explains why cramming, re-reading and blocked practice feel effective and are not, and why a lesson that looks fluent may leave little behind.
- It is a principle for designing practice and review, not for first explanations of complex new material.

## What the evidence says

Robert Bjork introduced the term in 1994; he and Elizabeth Bjork have developed it since at UCLA. Their underlying 'new theory of disuse' distinguishes **storage strength** (how well embedded a memory is) from **retrieval strength** (how accessible it is now). Retrieving something when retrieval strength has fallen, that is when it is partly forgotten, produces a bigger gain in storage strength than retrieving it when it is fresh. Easy, immediate repetition therefore adds little.

The main desirable difficulties are:

- **Spacing** study or practice over time.
- **Interleaving** different problem types or topics, not blocking them.
- **Testing** (retrieval practice) in place of re-presentation.
- **Generation**: producing an answer, prediction or solution, even with errors, before receiving it.
- **Variation**: practising in varied contexts, formats or conditions.
- **Reducing or delaying feedback** in some skill-learning contexts, so learners do not become dependent on it.

Evidence for the first three is extensive in laboratory research, and reasonably supportive in classrooms for retrieval and spacing (see those entries). Evidence for generation and variation in school classrooms is thinner. A related proposal, that hard-to-read fonts improve retention ('disfluency'), has largely failed to replicate and should not be used.

The Bjorks also document a metacognitive problem: learners judge their learning by how fluent it feels, so they prefer massed practice and re-reading and rate them as more effective even after experiencing better results from the alternatives.

## In the classroom

- **Judge learning later, not at the end of the lesson.** A correct plenary answer shows performance. Check again next week, without cues.
- **Let forgetting begin before reviewing.** Quiz Year 8 on rivers three weeks after the unit, not the next day only.
- **Ask before telling.** 'Predict what happens to the current when I add a second bulb in parallel, and write why.' A wrong prediction followed by feedback is productive, provided feedback does follow.
- **Mix and vary practice.** After a block on simultaneous equations, set questions in different forms (worded, graphical, algebraic) among other topics. In MFL, practise the same tense across speaking, translation and free writing.
- **Remove cues gradually.** First quiz with the knowledge organiser open, then closed; first with sentence starters, then without.
- **Explain it to pupils.** Year 10 and 11 pupils who know why self-testing feels worse than highlighting are more likely to persist. Show them their own scores from a re-read versus self-test comparison if you can.
- **Protect success.** If most of the class cannot do the task even with effort, the difficulty is undesirable: go back to modelling and guided practice.

## For leaders

- The learning–performance distinction should shape **how lessons are evaluated**. Rapid visible 'progress in 20 minutes' is not evidence of learning; Ofsted dropped the expectation of demonstrating progress within a lesson years ago, and observation proformas should not reintroduce it.
- Build delayed, cumulative assessment into the calendar so that departments get information about retention.
- Expect some teacher and pupil resistance: the approaches feel worse. Share the reasoning with staff, pupils and parents.
- Low cost; the work is redesigning practice, homework and assessment.

## Cautions and critiques

- **Which difficulties, for whom?** The concept is defined partly by its outcome: a difficulty is desirable if it helps. That makes it hard to predict in advance and open to circular use.
- **Tension with cognitive load theory.** For novices meeting complex material, added difficulty can overload working memory. A common reconciliation is that load should be reduced during initial instruction and difficulty introduced during practice of material that is already partly learned.
- **Lab-to-classroom transfer.** The EEF's 2021 review found that classroom evidence for several of these strategies is limited by subject and age, especially for interleaving outside maths.
- **Motivation.** Repeated struggle and lower in-lesson scores can reduce confidence, particularly for pupils with low prior attainment, unless success is engineered and the purpose explained.
- **Misuse as justification.** 'Desirable difficulty' has been used to defend unclear explanations and under-scaffolded tasks. The Bjorks' work does not support making first teaching confusing.
