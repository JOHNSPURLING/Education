## At a glance

- Explicit instruction means the teacher tells and shows pupils what they need to know and do, then moves them through guided practice to independent practice, checking understanding throughout. 'I do, we do, you do' is the common shorthand.
- It is not lecturing. It is highly interactive: frequent questions, whole-class responses and practice.
- The evidence that novices learn new, complex material better with full guidance than by discovering it is strong and comes from several research traditions.
- As pupils gain expertise, guidance should be faded; fully guided teaching of experts can be redundant.
- It is one of the EEF's 'five-a-day' strategies for pupils with SEND and is embedded in the DfE's initial teacher training and early career framework (in force from September 2025).
- Lower-case 'explicit' or 'direct' instruction is a broad approach; capitalised Direct Instruction is Engelmann's scripted programmes (see the separate entry).

## What the evidence says

Three strands of evidence point the same way:

- **Classroom observation research** (summarised by Rosenshine): teachers whose classes gained most presented in small steps, modelled, questioned heavily and guided practice before independent work.
- **Cognitive load research.** Kirschner, Sweller and Clark (2006) argued that minimally guided approaches (pure discovery, unstructured problem-based or inquiry learning) overload novices' working memory, and that decades of controlled studies favour strong guidance. Their 2012 *American Educator* article restates the case for teachers.
- **Programme evaluations and reviews**, including the Direct Instruction literature and reviews of interventions for pupils with learning difficulties, where explicit teaching repeatedly features in effective programmes. The EEF's guidance on SEND in mainstream schools draws on this.

The EEF describes explicit instruction as teacher-led: clear explanation, modelling and frequent checks, then guided practice, then independent practice. The DfE framework for new teachers includes the same sequence under 'classroom practice' and 'how pupils learn'.

There is no single EEF Toolkit 'explicit instruction' strand with a months-of-progress figure; do not quote one.

## In the classroom

A Year 9 English lesson on writing an analytical paragraph:

1. **State the goal and activate prior knowledge.** 'Today you will write a paragraph that embeds a quotation and explains a writer's choice.' Quick retrieval of what 'connotation' means.
2. **Explain in small steps.** Teach embedding a quotation before teaching analysis of a single word.
3. **I do.** Write a paragraph live under the visualiser, thinking aloud: 'I'm choosing "prowled" because the verb does the work; I won't copy the whole sentence.'
4. **Show a non-example.** A weak paragraph with a dropped-in quotation; pupils say what is wrong.
5. **We do.** Co-construct a second paragraph on a new quotation by cold call; pupils write each sentence on mini-whiteboards before you take one.
6. **Check.** Every pupil writes one embedded quotation and holds it up. If many are wrong, re-model. If a few are, pull a small group while others start.
7. **You do.** Independent paragraph, in silence, with a sentence-starter scaffold available and then withdrawn next lesson.
8. **Review** next lesson and again later in the term.

Common slips: explanations that run for 20 minutes without pupils doing anything; skipping 'we do'; and releasing to independent work on the strength of two confident volunteers.

## For leaders

- Explicit instruction is a sensible **default for new content**, particularly at Key Stage 3 and for pupils with gaps in prior knowledge. Policy should say 'default', not 'only'.
- It depends on **curriculum sequencing and subject knowledge**: teachers cannot break content into steps they have not analysed. Give departments planning time for shared explanations, models and practice sets.
- Develop it through coaching on specific moves (clear explanation, modelling, checking) and avoid prescribing timings for each phase.
- Cost is low. Mini-whiteboards and visualisers are the main kit.
- Watch for over-scaffolding: monitor whether pupils in Years 10–13 are writing and solving independently at length.

## Cautions and critiques

- **The 2006 paper was contested.** Hmelo-Silver, Duncan and Chinn (2007) replied that well-designed problem-based and inquiry learning is heavily scaffolded, not 'minimally guided', so the paper attacked a straw man. A more recent exchange (Zhang, Kirschner, Cobern and Sweller 2022; de Jong and colleagues 2023) ended with the latter arguing for combining inquiry and direct instruction.
- **Sequencing may be open.** Work on 'productive failure' (Manu Kapur) finds that, for some conceptual content, attempting problems before instruction can aid later understanding. Explicit instruction still follows; the dispute is about order.
- **Expertise reversal.** Heavy guidance helps novices and can hinder more advanced learners, which matters for sixth-form teaching.
- **Aims beyond acquisition.** Critics argue that disciplinary practices such as designing an investigation, sustained argument or making in the arts need extended, less-structured practice, and that these can be squeezed out.
- **Poor versions.** Teacher talk without checking, or scripted slides delivered identically to every class, is not what the research tested.
