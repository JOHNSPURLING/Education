## At a glance

- A free evidence review (June 2020) by Rob Coe, C.J. Rauch, Stuart Kime and Dan Singleton, published by Evidence Based Education (EBE) with Cambridge Assessment International Education.
- It answers one question: what are the best bets for teachers to work on to improve pupil learning?
- The answer is a **Model for Great Teaching**: four dimensions, broken into 17 elements.
- The model is a curriculum for teacher learning, not a rubric for grading lessons.
- 'Great Teaching Toolkit' also names EBE's paid online platform (courses, pupil surveys and feedback tools) built on the review. The review can be used on its own.

## What the evidence says

The authors reviewed existing research and teaching frameworks (EBE describes it as distilling over 100 frameworks and studies) and looked for teacher knowledge and behaviours with the strongest links to pupil outcomes. The review was commented on by an international group of advisers before publication. It builds on the 2014 Sutton Trust report *What Makes Great Teaching?*, which Coe also led.

The four dimensions and their elements are:

| Dimension | Elements |
|---|---|
| **1. Understanding the content** | Deep subject knowledge; knowledge of curriculum sequencing; knowledge of tasks, assessments and explanations; knowledge of common misconceptions and sticking points |
| **2. Creating a supportive environment** | Respectful teacher–pupil relationships; a positive climate among pupils; promoting motivation; high expectations with challenge and trust |
| **3. Maximising opportunity to learn** | Managing time and resources efficiently; clear, consistently applied rules and expectations; preventing and responding to disruption |
| **4. Activating hard thinking** | Structuring; explaining; questioning; interacting (feedback); embedding (practice and retrieval); activating (metacognition and independence) |

Two features distinguish it from lists such as Rosenshine's. First, it gives equal standing to subject knowledge, climate and behaviour, not only instruction. Second, it is explicit that the evidence varies: the authors describe the elements as 'best bets' and acknowledge that much underlying research is correlational, that the links between teacher behaviours and outcomes are modest, and that the model will need revising.

**How it differs from the EEF Toolkit.** The EEF Toolkit ranks school-level approaches by average impact, cost and evidence strength, for spending decisions. The Great Teaching Toolkit gives no months-of-progress figures; it maps the components of individual classroom practice for professional development.

## In the classroom

For an individual secondary teacher, the model works as a self-audit followed by narrow, deliberate improvement:

1. **Pick one element.** For example 4.3 Questioning, or 3.1 managing time (starts and transitions).
2. **Get a baseline.** Film ten minutes of a lesson, ask a colleague to tally who answers questions, or use a short pupil survey. In a Year 10 history lesson, a tally might show that eight pupils answered every question.
3. **Choose one specific technique** linked to the element, such as cold calling with wait time, or a hinge question before independent work.
4. **Practise it for several weeks** with the same class, with feedback from a coach or peer.
5. **Re-measure**, then move on.

The 'understanding the content' dimension is a prompt for departments: time spent in department meetings collecting the common misconceptions for the next unit (for example 'heavier objects fall faster', or 'the Treaty of Versailles caused the Second World War') directly builds element 1.4.

## For leaders

- Use the model to **structure a professional development curriculum** and to check its balance. Many school programmes sit almost entirely in dimension 4 and neglect subject knowledge.
- It supports **teacher choice**: staff select an element relevant to their classes, within a common framework. This fits instructional coaching well.
- **Do not turn the 17 elements into an observation grid.** The authors argue the model is for development; the research on grading lessons by observation (which Coe has long criticised) shows poor reliability.
- The EBE platform is a subscription product. Not verified: current pricing, course list and the detail of its feedback tools; check EBE's site. No independent trial of the platform's impact on pupil attainment was found while researching this entry.
- The review itself is free, so the cost of using the model is staff time.

## Cautions and critiques

- **Best bets, not proven recipes.** Evidence that a behaviour is associated with better outcomes is not evidence that training teachers in it will raise attainment.
- **Generic by design.** The elements are subject-neutral; how 'explaining' or 'embedding' look in music, mathematics or English differs sharply, and the review leaves that translation to teachers.
- **Commercial interest.** EBE both wrote the review and sells the platform built on it. The review is transparent about its method, but leaders should weigh that when buying.
- **Measurement.** Pupil surveys and classroom observation tools give noisy data on individual teachers; they are suitable for formative feedback and unsuitable for appraisal.
- **Breadth.** Seventeen elements is a lot. Without prioritisation the model becomes another list.
