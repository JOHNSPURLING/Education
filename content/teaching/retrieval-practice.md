## At a glance

- Bringing information to mind from memory (the 'testing effect') makes it more retrievable later than re-reading or highlighting the same material.
- It works best when it is effortful but mostly successful, low- or no-stakes, involves every pupil, and is followed by feedback.
- Dunlosky and colleagues (2013) rated practice testing as one of only two 'high utility' study techniques, with distributed practice.
- A 2021 systematic review of classroom studies found benefits in the majority of experiments, though settings and methods varied widely.
- Retrieval is for securing what has been taught. It is not a way of teaching new material, and factual recall quizzes alone will not build understanding or extended writing.

## What the evidence says

Laboratory studies over more than a century, and most famously Roediger and Karpicke's 2006 experiments, show that learners who test themselves remember more a week later than those who re-study, even though re-study feels more effective and produces better scores on an immediate test.

**Dunlosky et al. (2013)** reviewed ten study techniques. Practice testing and distributed practice were rated high utility because they worked across ages, materials and outcome measures. Re-reading, highlighting and summarising, the techniques pupils most often choose, were rated low.

**Agarwal, Nunes and Blunt (2021)** reviewed 50 classroom experiments (49 effect sizes, about 5,400 pupils and students). Most showed medium or large benefits compared with business-as-usual activities such as re-study. The authors concluded that teachers should use retrieval and worry less about its precise format or timing.

**The EEF's 2021 cognitive science review** was more guarded. It judged the evidence for retrieval practice in classrooms as promising, but noted that many studies were short, researcher-delivered, used recall of facts as the outcome, and were concentrated in particular subjects and ages. It found less evidence about retrieval for complex material and about how quizzing works when designed and delivered by teachers over a whole course.

Other points with reasonable support: free recall and short-answer formats are generally at least as effective as multiple choice; feedback increases the benefit, especially when recall fails; and retrieval combined with spacing is stronger than either alone.

## In the classroom

- **Starter quiz with a spread of content.** Six questions: two from last lesson, two from last unit, two from last year. Pupils answer in silence from memory, then self-mark in a different colour. Keep it to about five to eight minutes including feedback.
- **Everyone retrieves.** Mini-whiteboards or written answers, not hands up. One pupil answering aloud means one pupil retrieving.
- **Brain dumps.** 'Write everything you remember about the causes of the First World War in four minutes', then compare with the knowledge organiser and add what was missed in another colour.
- **Vary the demand.** Move beyond definitions: 'Give two differences between mitosis and meiosis', 'Which of these three quotations best shows Macbeth's guilt, and why?', a labelled diagram from memory, a five-step maths problem from last term.
- **Use the results.** If half the class miss a question, reteach it and put it in next week's quiz. A quiz whose results change nothing is a ritual.
- **Aim for high, not perfect, success.** If most pupils score under half, the gap since teaching was too long or the first teaching was not secure: reteach rather than re-quiz.
- **Teach pupils to do it themselves.** Flashcards used properly (answer before turning, shuffle, separate known from unknown), 'look, cover, write, check', and past questions from memory. Model this explicitly from Year 7; do not assume it by Year 11.

## For leaders

- Make retrieval a **curriculum decision**: departments should specify what knowledge must be retained and when it will be revisited. Otherwise quizzes default to last lesson's trivia.
- Avoid mandating a format ('five-a-day in every lesson'). The 2021 review suggests format matters less than regular, well-targeted use; PE, art and drama need their own versions.
- Do not collect quiz scores centrally. Recording turns a low-stakes learning activity into a high-stakes assessment and adds workload.
- Shared, pre-written quiz banks per unit are the main workload saver.
- Include study-skills teaching for pupils and information for parents on why self-testing beats re-reading.

## Cautions and critiques

- **Lab-to-classroom transfer.** The EEF review's central caution: effects demonstrated with word lists or short texts over days do not guarantee the same gains from teacher-made quizzes across a GCSE course.
- **Shallow recall.** Quizzing isolated facts can crowd out connected explanation. Evidence that retrieval of facts transfers to new, inferential questions is mixed.
- **Lethal mutation.** A ten-minute starter, unrelated to the lesson, eating a sixth of curriculum time, with no feedback and no reteaching.
- **Low success demotivates.** Pupils who repeatedly fail quizzes, including some with SEND or large knowledge gaps, may gain little without cues, scaffolds or reteaching.
- **Effect-size comparisons** across such varied studies have been criticised, a limitation Tes's coverage of the 2021 review also notes.
- **Anxiety.** Keep stakes genuinely low; evidence suggests routine low-stakes quizzing tends to reduce test anxiety over time, but public scoring can do the opposite.
