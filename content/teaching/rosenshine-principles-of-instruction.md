## At a glance

- Ten principles, published in *American Educator* in spring 2012 and adapted from a 2010 pamphlet Rosenshine wrote for the International Academy of Education/UNESCO.
- They describe how effective teachers introduce and secure **new material**: review, present in small steps, question, model, guide practice, check understanding, keep success high, scaffold, set independent practice, review again.
- They draw on three bodies of research: cognitive science, classroom studies of 'master' teachers, and studies of cognitive supports such as scaffolds and models.
- In England they became very widely used after Tom Sherrington's 2019 book grouped them into four strands. They sit comfortably with the DfE's initial teacher training and early career framework.
- They are a description of instruction, not a lesson plan, a checklist for observers or a full theory of teaching.

## What the evidence says

The ten principles are:

1. Begin the lesson with a short review of previous learning.
2. Present new material in small steps, with practice after each step.
3. Ask a large number of questions and check the responses of all pupils.
4. Provide models (including worked examples and thinking aloud).
5. Guide pupil practice.
6. Check for pupil understanding.
7. Obtain a high success rate.
8. Provide scaffolds for difficult tasks.
9. Require and monitor independent practice.
10. Engage pupils in weekly and monthly review.

The article also lists 17 'instructional procedures', which is why some summaries refer to 17 principles; they are the same ideas at a finer grain.

Much of the classroom evidence comes from 'process–product' studies of the 1960s–1980s, which observed teachers whose classes made larger gains on tests and then, in some cases, trained other teachers in those behaviours experimentally. Rosenshine reports, for example, that the more effective teachers spent longer on guided practice and questioning before releasing pupils to work alone, and suggests a success rate of around 80% during practice as a rough optimum: high enough that pupils are not rehearsing errors, low enough that the work is still demanding. The principles align with later findings on retrieval, worked examples and cognitive load, although Rosenshine wrote them as a synthesis rather than a test of those theories.

Sherrington's four strands are a useful way to hold them: **sequencing concepts and modelling** (2, 4, 8), **questioning** (3, 6), **reviewing material** (1, 10) and **stages of practice** (5, 7, 9).

## In the classroom

A Year 8 science lesson on balancing equations might run:

- **Review (5 minutes).** Five questions on silent mini-whiteboards: two from last lesson (formulae of common compounds), two from last term, one from last year. Reteach the one most got wrong.
- **Small steps.** Teach 'count the atoms on each side' before 'add coefficients'. Pupils practise counting on three examples before moving on.
- **Model.** Work one example under the visualiser, narrating decisions ('I never change the small numbers, because that changes the substance').
- **Guided practice.** A second example with the class supplying each step by cold call; a third on whiteboards, 'show me in 3, 2, 1'.
- **Check.** Do not ask 'does everyone get it?'. Scan the boards; if fewer than about four in five are correct, re-model rather than moving on.
- **Scaffold.** A tally-table template for those who need it, removed after a few questions.
- **Independent practice.** Ten questions in silence, the first few close to the modelled examples; the teacher circulates and marks a target group.
- **Later review.** The same content reappears in next week's starter and a half-termly quiz.

Not every lesson contains all ten. A lesson that is mostly extended writing or a practical is still consistent with the principles if the earlier lessons did the modelling and guided practice.

## For leaders

- Use the principles as a **shared language** for professional development, not as a lesson observation proforma. Scoring lessons against ten boxes produces performed starters and token questioning.
- Ask subject leaders to translate each principle into their subject. 'Review' in art or PE does not look like a five-question quiz.
- The highest-leverage strands for most departments are guided practice and checking the whole class before independent work. Target coaching there.
- Cost is low: the article is free and the main investment is training and coaching time.

## Cautions and critiques

- **Age and nature of the evidence.** Much of it is correlational, American, primary-phase and focused on well-structured content such as arithmetic and reading. Rosenshine himself noted that explicit teaching procedures are most applicable to well-structured knowledge and skills.
- **Not a complete account.** The principles say little about curriculum content, motivation, relationships, behaviour or subject-specific pedagogy. Sherrington and others stress that they should be read alongside a strong curriculum.
- **Lethal mutations.** Common distortions include: a retrieval quiz bolted onto every lesson regardless of content; the '80%' figure treated as a target grade; and whole-school lesson structures that force all ten into every hour.
- **Subject fit.** Teachers of arts, humanities and practical subjects argue that the principles need substantial adaptation where the aim is interpretation, performance or open-ended making.
