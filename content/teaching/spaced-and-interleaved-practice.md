## At a glance

- **Spacing**: returning to material after a gap produces better long-term retention than the same time spent in one block ('massing' or cramming).
- **Interleaving**: mixing different types of problem within a practice set, so pupils must choose the method, beats practising one type at a time ('blocking') for later performance, at least in mathematics.
- Both make practice feel harder and reduce performance in the moment. Pupils and teachers often misread that as failure.
- Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated distributed practice 'high utility' and interleaved practice 'moderate utility'.
- The EEF's 2021 review found classroom evidence for spacing positive but limited, and evidence for interleaving confined almost entirely to maths.
- Block first, then mix: pupils need initial instruction and some blocked practice before interleaving helps.

## What the evidence says

**Spacing.** The spacing effect has been replicated since Ebbinghaus in the 1880s, and the Bjorks describe it as among the oldest and most robust findings in research on learning. Reviews suggest the best gap grows with the length of time you want material retained: days or weeks for an end-of-term test, longer for an examination a year away. There is no single optimal schedule to prescribe.

**Interleaving.** The strongest classroom evidence comes from Doug Rohrer and colleagues' studies of school mathematics in the United States, including a large randomised study with pupils aged about 12–13 that found substantially higher scores on a delayed test for classes given interleaved practice sets (Not verified against the paper for this entry: exact sample and effect size). The proposed mechanism is discrimination: in a blocked set every question uses the same method, so pupils never practise the step examinations demand, which is recognising what kind of problem this is.

**The EEF review (2021)** concluded that spacing in classrooms has a small number of studies with mostly positive results, varying greatly in the gaps used; and that interleaving research is largely limited to maths, so teachers in other subjects have little direct evidence to go on. It also warned against confusing interleaving of similar, confusable problem types with simply switching between unrelated topics.

## In the classroom

**Spacing**

- **Review cycles built into schemes of work.** Starter quizzes and homework draw on last week, last term and last year, on a planned schedule.
- **Lagged homework.** Set homework on the topic taught three weeks ago, not today's. A Year 9 maths homework during a geometry unit might be 70% earlier topics (fractions, ratio, equations) and 30% current.
- **Cumulative assessments.** End-of-unit tests include a section on prior units, so pupils have a reason to keep earlier content alive.
- **Split revision.** Four 20-minute sessions on electrolysis across a fortnight beat one 80-minute session. Give Year 11 a revision timetable that returns to each topic at least three times.
- **Revisit through the curriculum.** A history curriculum that returns to 'parliament and monarchy' in Years 7, 8 and 9 spaces the concept, not just the facts.

**Interleaving**

- **Mixed practice sets in maths and sciences.** After teaching area of a circle, a set includes circumference, area of sectors and compound shapes from earlier, so pupils must select the formula. In physics, mix the equations for speed, acceleration and force.
- **Confusable concepts side by side** in other subjects: metaphor versus personification versus pathetic fallacy in English; preterite versus imperfect in Spanish; ionic versus covalent bonding. The value is in telling them apart.
- **Sequence**: worked examples and a short blocked set first; interleave once pupils can do each type with support.
- **Tell pupils why.** 'This will feel harder and you will make more mistakes than yesterday. That is expected, and it is what makes it stick.'

## For leaders

- Spacing is largely a **curriculum and assessment design** matter: schemes of work, homework policy and assessment calendars determine whether it happens. Individual teachers cannot do it alone.
- Ask each department how prior content is revisited, and look at homework and assessment papers for evidence, not lesson plans.
- Interleaving should be expected in maths and encouraged, cautiously, where subjects have confusable procedures or concepts. Do not mandate it across the school.
- Avoid 'interleaved curricula' that chop topics into fragments taught in rotation. That is not what the research tested and it can damage coherence.
- Costs are low: the work is in rewriting practice sets and homework.

## Cautions and critiques

- **Thin classroom evidence outside maths and science.** This is the EEF review's main message. Laboratory results with word lists or picture categories do not show that mixing topics helps in English literature or geography.
- **Short-term dip.** Lower scores during practice can demotivate, and lesson observers may mistake the struggle for weak teaching.
- **Novices and low prior attainment.** Interleaving too early, before any method is secure, raises load and error rates without the benefit.
- **Unknown optimal gaps.** Published rules of thumb for spacing intervals come largely from laboratory studies of fact learning.
- **Curriculum time.** Spacing means less time on new content now; over-zealous review can slow coverage in content-heavy GCSEs.
- **Terminology drift.** 'Interleaving' is often used in schools to mean a spiral or rotating topic order. Be precise about which is meant.
