## At a glance

- Rosenshine's ten principles describe what the most effective teachers were observed to do, cross-checked against cognitive science and research on scaffolds.
- The essence: review often, teach in small steps, model, ask lots of questions, check everyone's understanding, guide practice until success is high, then build independent fluency.
- The original article is the primary source. It is short, free and readable; teachers should read it rather than a poster summary.
- The principles are a description of effective explicit teaching, not a lesson plan, and Rosenshine did not present them as a checklist.
- The evidence base is strongest for well-structured content and novices, and much of the underlying classroom research is from primary reading and mathematics.

## Who they are

Barak Rosenshine (1930–2017) taught history in Chicago schools before completing a doctorate at Stanford and spending his academic career in educational psychology at the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign, where he became emeritus professor. From the late 1960s he was a central figure in 'process-product' research: studies that observed what teachers did (process) and related it to pupils' achievement gains (product). He also wrote on teacher effects, direct instruction, reciprocal teaching and the teaching of cognitive strategies. He died in 2017, shortly before his principles became ubiquitous in English schools, largely through Tom Sherrington's 2019 book and the wider researchED movement.

## Core ideas

**Where the principles came from.** Rosenshine drew on three bodies of research that, he argued, do not contradict each other:

1. *Cognitive science*: how the brain acquires and uses information, especially the limits of working memory and the role of well-connected background knowledge.
2. *Classroom practices of master teachers*: observational studies of teachers whose classes made the largest gains.
3. *Cognitive supports*: research on scaffolds, models and thinking aloud that help pupils learn complex tasks.

The principles appeared first as a pamphlet for the International Academy of Education (2010) and then in revised form in *American Educator* (Spring 2012). They develop earlier lists, notably the six 'teaching functions' he set out with Robert Stevens in 1986.

**The ten principles.**

1. Begin a lesson with a short review of previous learning.
2. Present new material in small steps, with pupil practice after each step.
3. Ask a large number of questions and check the responses of all pupils.
4. Provide models, including worked examples and thinking aloud.
5. Guide pupil practice.
6. Check for pupil understanding.
7. Obtain a high success rate.
8. Provide scaffolds for difficult tasks.
9. Require and monitor independent practice.
10. Engage pupils in weekly and monthly review.

**The seventeen procedures.** The article also lists seventeen 'instructional procedures'. These overlap with the ten and add detail: for example, limiting the amount of material pupils receive at one time, giving clear and detailed instructions, thinking aloud and modelling steps, asking pupils to explain what they have learned, providing systematic feedback and corrections, reteaching when necessary, and preparing pupils for independent practice. This is why some summaries refer to 'seventeen principles'.

**Details that are often lost.**

- *Success rate.* Rosenshine reported that the most effective teachers obtained a success rate of around 80% during guided practice: high enough to show secure learning, with enough challenge to avoid triviality.
- *Time on guided practice.* More effective teachers spent longer presenting, questioning and guiding practice before releasing pupils to work alone. Less effective teachers gave short presentations and long worksheets, so pupils practised errors.
- *Checking everyone.* The least effective teachers asked 'Are there any questions?' and took silence as understanding. Effective teachers asked pupils to explain, summarise or show working.
- *Scaffolds are temporary.* Cue cards, checklists, partially completed examples and anticipating errors are withdrawn as competence grows: a cognitive apprenticeship.
- *Overlearning.* Independent practice continues beyond first success, to build automaticity.

## Putting it into practice

For a classroom teacher:

- **Start with five minutes of review** of material pupils will need today and material from weeks ago. Make everyone answer in writing.
- **Chunk the explanation.** Teach one step, model it, then ask every pupil to try a near-identical example before the next step.
- **Script the questions.** Plan process questions ('How did you get that?', 'Why is the other option wrong?') and decide how every pupil will respond.
- **Stay in guided practice longer.** Do not set independent work until most of the class is getting most items right. If they are not, reteach; do not circulate and patch pupils one at a time.
- **Fade the scaffold.** Move from a full worked example, to a partially completed one, to a prompt card, to nothing.
- **Build weekly and monthly review** into the scheme of work, not as an afterthought.

For leaders:

- Give staff the original twelve-page article and discuss what the principles look like in each subject. Practice in art, PE and English literature will not look like practice in mathematics.
- Do not require all ten principles in every lesson or use them as an observation checklist. They describe teaching over a sequence of lessons.
- Use them as a shared vocabulary for coaching conversations about one specific area at a time.

## Critiques and debate

- **Narrow evidence base.** Much of the process-product research was conducted in the 1970s and 1980s in American primary classrooms, teaching basic skills, with standardised tests as the outcome. Critics question how far the findings transfer to, for example, A-level literature or design subjects. Rosenshine himself distinguished between well-structured and less-structured tasks and recommended scaffolds, not step-by-step procedures, for the latter.
- **Correlational roots.** Process-product studies are largely correlational, though Rosenshine noted that experimental studies training teachers in the methods found gains.
- **Lethal mutations.** Writers including Tom Sherrington and Christine Counsell have warned that the principles have been turned into generic lesson templates and monitoring forms. Counsell and other subject specialists argue that they say nothing about *what* is taught and can crowd out subject-specific thinking.
- **Not a theory of curriculum or motivation.** The principles are silent on curriculum content, relationships, behaviour and motivation, so they cannot serve as a complete model of teaching.
- **Ideological objection.** Advocates of enquiry-based learning see the principles as privileging transmission. Supporters respond that the principles end with independent practice and that the evidence favours explicit teaching for novices.
