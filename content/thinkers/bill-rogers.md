## At a glance

- Rogers gives teachers a **language of discipline**: short, respectful, rehearsed phrases that correct behaviour without escalating it.
- Core principle: respond with the **least intrusive** intervention that will work, and move up only as needed.
- Key techniques: tactical ignoring, description of reality, rule reminders, take-up time, partial agreement, directed choices, and separating **primary** from **secondary** behaviours.
- The first weeks with a class (the **establishment phase**) are when rights, rules and routines are taught and practised.
- Consequences matter, but it is the **certainty** of follow-up, not its severity, that changes behaviour.
- Best used as a classroom-level skill set inside a clear whole-school system; it is not itself a school policy.

## Who they are

Dr Bill Rogers is an Australian education consultant and former teacher who has taught in primary and secondary schools. According to his publisher's biography, in 1985 he took up a consultancy post with the Victorian education ministry to promote whole-school approaches to discipline and student welfare. His website describes him as an Honorary Fellow of the Melbourne Graduate School of Education and an Honorary Life Fellow of Leeds Trinity University, and he was an adjunct professor of education at Griffith University for eight years from 2000. He has lectured in UK schools and universities for decades, and his website advertised a UK and Europe lecture tour for autumn 2026. His filmed classroom demonstrations and books are staples of initial teacher training in England.

## Core ideas

**Rights, responsibilities, rules and routines (the 4Rs).** Classroom discipline rests on shared rights: to learn, to teach, to feel safe and to be treated with respect. Rules protect those rights; responsibilities go with them; routines make them habitual. Rogers advises a small number of positively worded rules, ideally discussed with the class.

**The establishment phase.** In the first lessons the teacher explicitly teaches entry, seating, how to get attention, noise levels, how to ask for help and how to leave. Rogers treats this as the highest-leverage period: habits set here are far harder to change later.

**Least-to-most intrusive.** Start with the lightest touch and step up:

- **Tactical ignoring** of minor attention-seeking or secondary behaviour while attending to on-task pupils. It is a deliberate choice, not avoidance, and is never used for unsafe, hostile or persistently disruptive behaviour.
- **Non-verbal cues**: a look, a gesture, moving nearer.
- **Description of reality**: say what you see and let the pupil work out the rest. 'A number of people are talking.'
- **Rule reminder**: 'What's our rule for asking questions?'
- **Direct instruction, ending in thanks**: 'Jake, facing this way, thanks.' 'Thanks' signals expectation of compliance more than 'please'.
- **Directed choice**: 'Phone in your bag or on my desk. Your choice.'
- **Clarifying a consequence**, immediate or deferred, and then following through.

**Take-up time.** After a direction, turn away and carry on teaching. The pupil can comply without losing face, and the teacher avoids a stand-off.

**Primary and secondary behaviours.** The primary behaviour is the thing that needs to change (talking, phone out). Secondary behaviours are the sighs, eye-rolls, muttering and last words that follow correction. Rogers's advice is to keep the focus on the primary behaviour and deal with the rest later, if at all.

**Partial agreement.** Acknowledge the pupil's point and redirect, rather than argue it. 'Maybe you were only asking about the work, and I need you facing the front, thanks.' He also uses 'when... then' in place of 'no, because': 'When you've finished the paragraph, then you can go.'

**Certainty not severity.** Consequences should be known in advance, related to the behaviour, reasonable and respectful. A brief after-class conversation that always happens is worth more than a harsh sanction that sometimes does.

**Repair and rebuild.** After a consequence, the teacher re-establishes the working relationship. Rogers describes the 'decisive' teacher as neither authoritarian nor indecisive: calm, clear, respectful and firm. He also stresses **colleague support**: no teacher should be left alone with a hard class.

## Putting it into practice

**Teachers**

- Script your five most common corrections and practise them aloud: a description of reality, a rule reminder, a direction ending in 'thanks', a directed choice, a partial agreement.
- Use the pupil's name, pause, give the direction, then look away and keep teaching. Count take-up time in seconds, not minutes; return and acknowledge compliance quietly.
- Correct privately where possible: move to the desk, lower your voice.
- Replace 'why' questions ('Why are you talking?') with 'what' questions ('What should you be doing now?').
- With a new class, plan the establishment phase as carefully as the content: teach each routine, practise it, and re-teach after holidays.
- For deferred consequences, say calmly 'I'll need to see you for a minute after the lesson' and then always do it.
- After a difficult lesson, start the next one fresh; greet the pupil normally.

**Leaders and mentors**

- Use Rogers's vocabulary as shared language in coaching early career teachers: observe and feed back on tone, take-up time and focus on primary behaviour.
- Rehearse scripts in training sessions; classroom language improves with practice, not reading.
- Make it normal to ask for help with a hard class, including planned colleague support such as a time-out arrangement with a neighbouring room.
- Check that the whole-school system supplies the certainty Rogers relies on: if deferred consequences are not backed up, the classroom techniques lose force.

## Critiques and debate

- **Practitioner craft, not trial evidence.** Rogers's approach draws on long classroom experience and psychological theory (he acknowledges Adlerian influences such as Rudolf Dreikurs). There are no large controlled trials of 'the Rogers method'. Its elements are, however, consistent with what the EEF's *Improving Behaviour in Schools* guidance says about classroom management.
- **Classroom, not system.** Advocates of whole-school behaviour systems, such as Tom Bennett, argue that individual teacher technique cannot compensate for weak school culture. Rogers does write about whole-school approaches, but schools mostly use his classroom material.
- **Tactical ignoring is easily misapplied.** Critics note that novices can slide into ignoring disruption that needs addressing, lowering expectations. Rogers himself limits the technique to low-level, non-disruptive behaviour.
- **Negotiation and firmness.** Some proponents of very high-expectation approaches see partial agreement and choices as conceding too much; relational practitioners see Rogers as close to their own position. In practice he sits between consequence-led and restorative camps and is cited approvingly by both.
- **Context.** The demonstrations are largely from Australian classrooms some years ago; tone and examples may need adapting, though the principles transfer well.
