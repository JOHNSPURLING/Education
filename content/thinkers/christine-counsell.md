## At a glance

- Counsell's central distinction: **substantive knowledge** (the established facts, concepts and accounts of a subject) and **disciplinary knowledge** (how the subject establishes, tests and revises that knowledge). Pupils need both.
- **Curriculum is content structured as narrative over time**: what pupils learn now only makes sense in light of what came before and prepares for what comes later.
- **Core and hinterland**: the residue you want pupils to retain depends on the rich surrounding material through which it is taught. Cut the hinterland and the core becomes brittle.
- The **curriculum is the progression model**: getting better at a subject means knowing more of its curriculum, not climbing generic skill ladders.
- Senior leaders cannot lead curriculum generically. They need enough understanding of each subject's structure to ask good questions of subject leaders.
- Her work is the intellectual backbone of much of the post-2019 'curriculum turn' in England, including Ofsted's language.

## Who they are

Christine Counsell was a secondary history teacher, head of department and deputy headteacher before leading the history PGCE at the University of Cambridge for 19 years. She was for many years an editor of the Historical Association's journal *Teaching History*, where she fostered a tradition of history teachers publishing their own curriculum research. From 2016 she was director of education at the Inspiration Trust, and she served on Ofsted's curriculum advisory group during development of the 2019 framework (dates and titles not verified against primary sources). She now works independently as a curriculum consultant and is co-founder, with Steve Mastin, of **Opening Worlds**, a knowledge-rich humanities programme for key stage 2. Her blog, *The Dignity of the Thing*, carried her influential 2018 posts on senior curriculum leadership.

## Core ideas

**Substantive and disciplinary knowledge.** Substantive knowledge is what the subject claims: that the Reformation happened and how, what photosynthesis is, what a sonnet does. Disciplinary knowledge is what pupils learn about how such claims come to be made: how historians use evidence and argue about causation, how scientists test hypotheses, how critics construct interpretations. Counsell insists this is **knowledge about the discipline**, taught explicitly, not a set of generic 'skills'. A curriculum with only substantive content presents knowledge as fixed; one that chases disciplinary 'skills' without content has nothing to think with.

**Curriculum as narrative.** A curriculum works like a story: early content establishes characters, settings and ideas that later content relies on. Sequence therefore matters in its own right. Asking 'why this, why now?' of any unit is asking what it does for the narrative: what earlier knowledge makes it accessible, and what later learning it makes possible.

**Core and hinterland.** The core is what pupils should remember long-term. The hinterland is the supporting material (stories, examples, context, texts) that makes the core meaningful and memorable. In her account, reducing a novel or a historical period to a list of key points for retrieval strips out exactly what gives those points meaning. The distinction is a warning against thin knowledge organisers as much as against content-free skills.

**The curriculum as the progression model.** With her colleague Michael Fordham, Counsell argues that progress means having learned the curriculum. Assessment should check whether pupils have secured the specific content taught, cumulatively, rather than assign generic levels or GCSE-style grades in key stage 3.

**Final performance as 'deceiver and guide'.** The end product (an essay, an exam answer) is a poor guide to what should be taught on the way. Fluent reading and writing at 16 are the indirect result of broad knowledge accumulated over years. Rehearsing the final performance early, such as GCSE question types in Year 7, mistakes the outcome for the route.

**Critique of genericism.** She argues that English school leadership became dominated by generic tools (lesson grades, skills ladders, data tracking, uniform teaching policies) that ignore how subjects differ. What counts as good questioning, practice or feedback depends on the subject.

**Subject communities and middle leaders.** Curriculum quality depends on subject leaders who are scholars of their subject's curriculum, connected to subject associations and to published teacher research. Senior leaders' job is to create the conditions: time, subject-specific professional development and intelligent line management.

## Putting it into practice

**Subject leaders**

- For each unit, list the substantive content pupils must retain and the disciplinary question the unit serves (for example, in history, a causation or interpretation enquiry). Check both are present.
- Trace one concept ('parliament', 'energy', 'metaphor') across five years. Where is it first met, where deepened, where relied on?
- Mark your core and your hinterland. Protect the hinterland when time is short; do not reduce the unit to the knowledge organiser.
- Rewrite key stage 3 assessment to test what was taught, cumulatively. Remove GCSE grade descriptors from Years 7 to 9.
- Join your subject association; read and discuss its journal in department time.

**Senior leaders**

- Learn the basic shape of each subject you line-manage. Useful questions, in the spirit of her leadership posts: What is cumulative in your subject and what is hierarchical? What does this unit depend on, and what depends on it? What disciplinary thinking are pupils being introduced to? What would you lose if you cut this text or topic?
- Stop imposing generic lesson structures or feedback rules that do not fit particular subjects; let departments specify what good practice looks like for them, and hold them to it.
- Give subject teams regular, protected time for curriculum development and subject-specific CPD.
- Use quality assurance to look at the curriculum as enacted (plans, pupils' work, conversations with pupils about what they remember), not at isolated lessons against a generic checklist.

## Critiques and debate

- **Subject range.** Counsell's examples come mostly from history and the humanities. The substantive/disciplinary distinction maps less neatly on to mathematics, modern languages, PE or the arts, and subject specialists have had to adapt or contest it. Ofsted's subject research reviews use varying terminology for this reason.
- **Knowledge-rich critiques.** Critics of the knowledge turn, including some academics in curriculum studies and voices associated with the 2024-25 Curriculum and Assessment Review debates, argue that it can overload content, privilege a traditional canon and under-value creativity, oracy and applied learning. Counsell's own emphasis on disciplinary knowledge and on diverse, rigorous content is partly an answer to the canon criticism, but the argument continues.
- **Who chooses the narrative?** If curriculum is narrative, selection matters. Scholars concerned with representation ask whose stories are told. Counsell has written in support of broader and more diverse history content, grounded in scholarship.
- **Risk of new orthodoxy.** Her vocabulary was absorbed quickly into inspection and school documents, sometimes as jargon ('intent statements', sequencing rationales) rather than thought. She has herself warned against proxies and compliance.
- **Demand on leaders.** Expecting senior leaders to understand every subject's structure is a high bar, especially in small schools. Trust-wide subject networks are one response.
- **Published evidence.** Her arguments are scholarly and theoretical, supported by a large body of teacher-authored research in history education, not by trials comparing curriculum models.
