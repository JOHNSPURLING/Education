## At a glance

- Willingham explains findings from cognitive psychology in terms teachers can use, and is careful about what the science does and does not justify.
- 'Memory is the residue of thought': pupils remember what they think about, so plan lessons around what pupils will be thinking, not what they will be doing.
- Thinking well depends on knowing things. Critical thinking and comprehension cannot be taught separately from subject knowledge.
- Pupils are more alike than different in how they learn. There is no good evidence for teaching to learning styles.
- Proficiency needs extended, spaced practice, and novices do not learn best by imitating what experts do.

## Who they are

Daniel T. Willingham is professor of psychology at the University of Virginia, where he has taught since 1992. He trained as a cognitive psychologist (PhD, Harvard) and researched the brain basis of learning and memory before turning, from around 2000, to the application of cognitive psychology to schooling. He has written the 'Ask the Cognitive Scientist' column for the American Federation of Teachers' magazine *American Educator* since 2002; many of his best-known articles for teachers appeared there. His work was taken up in England in the 2010s by the knowledge-rich curriculum movement and was publicly cited by ministers including Nick Gibb.

## Core ideas

*Why Don't Students Like School?* (2009; second edition 2021) is organised as a set of questions, each answered by a cognitive principle. In summary:

1. **People are curious but not naturally good at thinking.** Thinking is slow and effortful, and the mind tries to avoid it by relying on memory. Pupils enjoy solving problems of the right difficulty: too easy or too hard and they disengage. This is his answer to the title question.
2. **Factual knowledge precedes skill.** Analysis, comprehension and problem-solving draw on knowledge in long-term memory. Background knowledge lets readers fill gaps in a text and lets thinkers 'chunk' information, freeing working memory. The more you know, the easier it is to learn more.
3. **Memory is the residue of thought.** What is stored is what the learner attended to and thought about. If pupils make a poster about the water cycle and spend the lesson thinking about lettering, they will remember lettering. He recommends reviewing every lesson plan for what pupils will actually think about, and notes the power of story structure (causality, conflict, complications, character) to make material memorable.
4. **We understand new things in terms of what we already know.** Abstract ideas are grasped through concrete examples and analogies. Understanding stays shallow at first and transfers poorly, so pupils need many examples and explicit comparison of their deep structure.
5. **Proficiency requires practice.** Practice makes basic processes automatic, protects against forgetting and aids transfer. It should be spread out over time.
6. **Novices do not think like experts.** Experts see deep structure and have vast, organised knowledge. Asking pupils to 'think like a historian' or 'be a scientist' before they have that knowledge mistakes the goal for the method. Pupils can comprehend expert knowledge well before they can create it.
7. **Children are more alike than different in how they learn.** Learners differ in ability and interest, but the theory that matching teaching to visual, auditory or kinaesthetic styles improves learning has not been supported. Choose the modality that suits the *content*.
8. **Intelligence can be changed through sustained hard work.** He supports praising effort and process, treating failure as a normal part of learning, and being honest that catching up means working harder. The second edition is more cautious about the strength of growth mindset intervention evidence.
9. **Technology.** A chapter new to the second edition argues that technology changes less about cognition than is claimed, that effects of specific tools are hard to predict, and that schools should evaluate products against a clear purpose.
10. **Teaching is a cognitive skill that improves with deliberate practice.** Experience alone is not enough; teachers need feedback, including observation and video, and to work on one thing at a time.

**Other themes.** In 'Critical thinking: why is it so hard to teach?' (2007) he argues that critical thinking is not a general skill but depends on domain knowledge and practice. *The Reading Mind* (2017) sets out the cognitive processes in reading, from decoding to comprehension, and stresses knowledge and vocabulary. *When Can You Trust the Experts?* (2012) gives educators a method for evaluating research claims. *Outsmart Your Brain* (2023) is a study guide for students on learning from lessons, reading, note-taking and revising.

## Putting it into practice

For a classroom teacher:

- **Audit the thinking.** For each task, write down what a pupil will be thinking about while doing it. If it is not the meaning of the content, change the task. Be wary of attention-grabbers and elaborate formats that become the thing remembered.
- **Pose the question before giving the answer.** Frame a lesson or unit around a puzzle that the content resolves, so the explanation matters to pupils.
- **Build and use knowledge.** Teach background knowledge before a demanding text, and make wide reading part of the curriculum.
- **Use several concrete examples** of each abstract idea and ask pupils to say what they have in common.
- **Space practice** of core procedures and facts across the year through starters, homework and cumulative quizzes.
- **Talk about effort and strategy**, not ability, when giving feedback.

For leaders:

- Remove learning styles questionnaires and VAK planning from documentation and training.
- Be sceptical of generic 'thinking skills' programmes that sit outside subjects.
- Give teachers structured opportunities for deliberate practice with feedback, such as video and coaching.
- Use his 'strip it, trace it, analyse it, should I do it?' approach when weighing a new product or initiative.

## Critiques and debate

- **Lab to classroom.** Willingham himself says that basic science describes the mind but cannot prescribe teaching; values and context also matter. Critics argue that popular use of his work in England has ignored that caution and treated cognitive principles as a complete pedagogy.
- **Knowledge versus skills.** Progressive educators argue that his account underplays enquiry, creativity and pupils' agency, and that 'knowledge first' can delay authentic disciplinary work. Supporters reply that he argues for the order of teaching, not against the goal.
- **Learning styles.** A minority of researchers hold that individual differences in preferred modes of learning deserve more study. The mainstream reviews (for example Pashler and colleagues, 2008) agree with Willingham that matching instruction to styles lacks evidence.
- **Motivation.** The book's account of why pupils dislike school is cognitive. Critics note that it says relatively little about relationships, belonging, relevance or the social causes of disengagement.
- **Breadth of evidence.** Much of the underlying research involves university students and short timescales, and the evidence base in some areas (for example mindset) has shifted since 2009, which the second edition partly reflects.
