## At a glance

- Wiliam's central claim: teaching is unpredictable, so teachers must keep finding out what pupils have actually learned and adjust. He calls assessment the bridge between teaching and learning.
- Formative assessment is a process defined by what you do with evidence, not a type of test.
- Five strategies organise the practice: clarify intentions, elicit evidence, give feedback that moves learning on, activate peers, activate pupils as owners of their learning.
- Feedback should cause thinking and be more work for the pupil than for the teacher.
- Improving the teachers a school already has, through sustained, collaborative habit change, matters more than structures or recruitment.

## Who they are

Dylan Wiliam taught mathematics and science in London secondary schools before moving into teacher education and research at what became King's College London. With Paul Black he wrote the 1998 research review 'Assessment and classroom learning' and its short summary for teachers, 'Inside the Black Box'. He later worked as a senior research director at the Educational Testing Service in the United States and was deputy director of the Institute of Education, University of London, from 2006 to 2010. He is emeritus professor of educational assessment at UCL Institute of Education and works internationally with teachers and school systems. Paul Black died in 2026; Wiliam wrote publicly about his legacy.

## Core ideas

**Inside the Black Box (1998).** Black and Wiliam reviewed around 250 studies and argued that strengthening formative assessment produces substantial learning gains, particularly for lower attainers. The classroom was the 'black box': policy focused on inputs and outputs and ignored what happens inside. Follow-up work with teachers (the King's-Medway-Oxfordshire project) produced 'Working Inside the Black Box' and *Assessment for Learning: Putting it into Practice*.

**The five key strategies.** First set out with Leahy, Lyon and Thompson in 2005, and built from three questions (where is the learner going, where are they now, how do they get there) and three agents (teacher, peer, learner):

1. Clarifying, sharing and understanding learning intentions and success criteria.
2. Engineering effective discussions, tasks and activities that elicit evidence of learning.
3. Providing feedback that moves learners forward.
4. Activating students as learning resources for one another.
5. Activating students as owners of their own learning.

**Hinge questions.** A question asked at a point in the lesson where the teacher must decide whether to move on. Wiliam's design rules: every pupil responds at once (mini-whiteboards, ABCD cards); pupils can answer in about a minute and the teacher can read the answers in seconds; and it should be very hard to get the right answer for the wrong reason, with each wrong option revealing a specific misconception.

**Short-cycle assessment.** The benefits come from minute-by-minute and day-by-day adjustment, not from termly tests labelled 'formative'. He also promotes 'no hands up, except to ask a question' with random selection so that participation is not voluntary.

**Feedback.** Drawing on Kluger and DeNisi's finding that a sizeable minority of feedback interventions lowered performance, he argues that feedback works only if pupils use it. Comment-only marking can beat grades because grades trigger ego-involvement. He suggests making feedback a task: for example, 'five of these are wrong; find them and fix them'.

**Teacher quality and teacher learning communities.** In *Creating the Schools Our Children Need* (2018) and *Leadership for Teacher Learning* (2016) he argues that many popular reforms (structural change, class-size cuts, replacing weak teachers, performance pay) are slow, costly or weakly evidenced. He summarises the alternative as 'love the one you're with': invest in existing teachers, alongside a knowledge-rich curriculum. Because the problem is changing habits, not gaining knowledge, he recommends teacher learning communities (TLCs): groups of 8–12 teachers meeting monthly for about 75 minutes over two years, each choosing one or two techniques, reporting back, observing peers and committing to next steps.

**Assessment more widely.** He has written on validity and reliability, argued that national curriculum levels were misused, and has been a prominent sceptic of fine-grained tracking data.

## Putting it into practice

For a classroom teacher:

- **Plan one hinge question per lesson.** Write it before the lesson, with distractors based on known misconceptions. Decide in advance what you will do if a third of the class gets it wrong.
- **Get a response from everyone.** Use mini-whiteboards or finger voting, and random selection for oral questions.
- **Turn feedback into detective work.** Instead of correcting each error, mark a dot in the margin of any line containing a mistake, or return work with comments on separate strips for pupils to match to the right piece.
- **Use exit tickets** to decide the opening of the next lesson, not to generate a grade.
- **Train peer assessment** with simple structures such as 'two stars and a wish' against shared criteria, and use pre-flight checklists before work is handed in.

For leaders:

- Run TLCs or an equivalent: voluntary choice of technique within the five strategies, small steps, peer accountability, protected time for at least two years. Stop doing something else to make room.
- Judge a feedback policy by what pupils do with feedback, not by the volume of written comments.
- Do not relabel frequent summative testing as assessment for learning.

## Critiques and debate

- **Size of the effect.** Black and Wiliam's early headline effect sizes (often quoted as 0.4–0.7) have been challenged. Randy Bennett (2011) argued that formative assessment was too loosely defined for a single effect size to mean much, and Kingston and Nash's 2011 meta-analysis reported a much smaller average effect (around 0.2). Wiliam has since said that he regrets how the effect sizes were used.
- **Implementation in England.** The national Assessment for Learning strategy of the 2000s is widely judged to have failed. Wiliam's own account is that it was implemented as levels-tracking and target-setting rather than responsive teaching. Others argue that the looseness of the original idea made such distortion likely.
- **Generic versus subject-specific.** Daisy Christodoulou, in *Making Good Progress?* (2017), supports formative assessment but argues that AfL in practice leaned on generic skills and exam-style tasks, and should instead rest on specific, well-sequenced knowledge and precise questions. Wiliam wrote the foreword and broadly accepts the point.
- **Learning intentions and peer assessment.** Some teachers find routinely shared objectives and success criteria formulaic, and peer assessment is hard to do well with novices who lack the knowledge to judge quality.
- **The EEF trial.** The independent evaluation of the Embedding Formative Assessment programme found a positive effect on GCSE attainment equivalent to about two months' progress. This is supportive but modest compared with early claims.
