## At a glance

- Sweller's claim: working memory is small and short-lived when handling new information; long-term memory is vast. Instruction should be designed around that bottleneck.
- Novices learn more from studying worked examples than from solving equivalent problems unaided.
- Reduce load that comes from poor presentation (split attention, redundancy); manage load that comes from the content itself by sequencing and pre-teaching.
- What helps novices can hinder experts (the expertise reversal effect), so guidance must be faded.
- The theory is influential and well supported in laboratory-style experiments, but load is hard to measure directly and critics question its falsifiability.

## Who they are

John Sweller is emeritus professor of educational psychology at the University of New South Wales, Sydney, where he spent most of his career. Cognitive load theory (CLT) grew out of his problem-solving experiments in the late 1970s and 1980s, in which students who solved many problems using means-ends analysis often failed to learn the underlying rule. His 1988 paper in *Cognitive Science* set out the explanation. He has since published several hundred papers and developed the theory with collaborators including Paul Chandler, Slava Kalyuga, Paul Ayres, Fred Paas, Jeroen van Merriënboer and Paul Kirschner. In England, CLT gained prominence in the late 2010s; Dylan Wiliam described it in 2017 as the most important thing for teachers to know, and it is referenced in Ofsted's research commentary for the 2019 inspection framework.

## Core ideas

**Human cognitive architecture.** Learning is a change in long-term memory. Long-term memory stores knowledge as schemas, which can be large yet are handled as single elements in working memory. Experts differ from novices mainly in the schemas they hold, not in general problem-solving skill. Working memory can hold only a few new elements at once, for seconds, but those limits largely disappear for information retrieved from long-term memory.

**Biologically primary and secondary knowledge.** Borrowing from David Geary, Sweller argues that humans acquire some things without instruction (speaking a first language, recognising faces, general problem-solving strategies) but that school subjects are 'biologically secondary' and need explicit teaching. He uses this to argue that generic thinking skills cannot usefully be taught.

**Types of load.**

- *Intrinsic load* comes from the material itself and the learner's prior knowledge.
- *Extraneous load* comes from the way the material is presented and does not contribute to learning.
- *Germane load* was added in 1998 to describe effort devoted to schema building. In later formulations Sweller treats it not as a separate source of load but as the working memory resources devoted to intrinsic load.

**Element interactivity.** The driver of intrinsic load is how many elements must be processed simultaneously. Learning a list of chemical symbols is low in interactivity: each can be learned alone. Balancing an equation is high: the elements only make sense together. Many CLT effects appear only with high element interactivity material.

**The main effects.**

- *Goal-free effect*: 'find what you can' produces more learning than 'find angle X', because it removes the means-ends search.
- *Worked example effect*: novices learn more from studying worked solutions than from solving the same problems.
- *Completion problem effect*: partially worked examples are a bridge to independent solving.
- *Split-attention effect*: learners lose capacity when they must integrate separated sources, such as a diagram and a distant key. Integrate labels into the diagram.
- *Redundancy effect*: adding information that duplicates what is already intelligible (for example, reading aloud text that is on the slide) harms learning.
- *Modality effect*: a diagram with spoken explanation can beat a diagram with written text, as it uses both visual and auditory channels.
- *Transient information effect*: long spoken or animated explanations vanish before they can be processed; a static, permanent record helps.
- *Expertise reversal effect*: as learners gain knowledge, worked examples and integrated guidance become redundant and can slow them down.
- *Guidance fading effect*: the practical upshot, moving from examples to completion problems to full problems.
- *Isolated elements effect*: for very complex content, teach elements separately first, accepting partial understanding, then combine.

**Against minimal guidance.** With Kirschner and Clark (2006) he argued that discovery, problem-based and enquiry learning ignore the architecture above and are less effective for novices than explicit instruction.

## Putting it into practice

For a classroom teacher:

- **Use example-problem pairs.** Show a worked example, then set a near-identical problem straight away. Then fade: give the first steps and ask pupils to finish.
- **Clean up slides and worksheets.** Put labels on the diagram, not in a key. Remove decorative images. Do not talk over dense text; either let pupils read in silence or say it and show only the diagram.
- **Leave the model visible.** Keep the worked example on the board during practice so pupils do not have to hold it in memory.
- **Pre-teach components.** Secure vocabulary and sub-procedures before the lesson that combines them.
- **Watch for reversal.** When a pupil or class is fluent, drop the scaffolds and set problems; continued worked examples will bore and slow them.

For leaders:

- Check shared resources and centrally produced slides for split attention and redundancy.
- Sequence curricula so that high-interactivity topics are preceded by their components.
- Treat CLT as a design principle for novices meeting complex material, not as a rule that every lesson must be teacher-led.

## Critiques and debate

- **Falsifiability and measurement.** Ton de Jong (2010) and others argue that, because the types of load cannot be measured independently (most studies use a single self-rating of mental effort), almost any result can be explained after the fact. Sweller's 2023 paper presents the theory's revisions after failed replications as a strength; critics see post hoc patching.
- **The status of germane load.** Its introduction and later demotion are cited as evidence of conceptual instability.
- **Productive failure.** Manu Kapur's research suggests that, for conceptual understanding, attempting problems *before* instruction can outperform instruction first. Sweller and colleagues dispute the design of some studies; the debate is live, and element interactivity is offered as a possible reconciliation.
- **Desirable difficulties.** The Bjorks' work shows that some added difficulty improves retention. Researchers on both sides have suggested that the two accounts apply at different stages or to material of different complexity.
- **Motivation and transfer.** CLT says little about motivation, emotion or collaboration, and most experiments use short interventions with well-structured mathematics, science or technical content. Its reach into the humanities and arts is argued by analogy more than tested.
- **Primary/secondary distinction.** Some psychologists consider Geary's distinction speculative and doubt it can bear the weight Sweller places on it.
- **Ideological critique.** Alfie Kohn and other progressive educators argue that CLT is used to legitimise teacher-directed instruction. David Didau and Paul Kirschner have published rebuttals.
