## At a glance

- Assertive Discipline is the source of the **rules + recognition + stepped consequences** model that most secondary behaviour policies still follow (C1, C2, C3 and so on).
- Its premise: teachers have a **right to teach** and pupils a **right to learn**, and no pupil may be allowed to prevent either.
- The teacher states expectations clearly, recognises pupils who meet them, and applies a **pre-published hierarchy of consequences** calmly and consistently.
- Canter distinguished **assertive** teachers from **non-assertive** (passive, pleading) and **hostile** (angry, sarcastic) ones.
- Later versions put much more weight on positive recognition, teaching routines and relationships, and dropped public devices such as names on the board.
- The research base is thin and the approach has been heavily criticised as controlling. Know it because your policy probably descends from it, and so you can spot its weaknesses.

## Who they are

Lee Canter is an American educator and trainer whose background was in social work and child guidance. With Marlene Canter, a former special education teacher, he published *Assertive Discipline* in 1976 and built a training company around it. Education Week reported that by 1989 some 500,000 teachers had been trained, making it probably the most widely used packaged discipline programme in American schools. It was imported into the UK in the early 1990s through commercial training and was widely adopted in English schools. Canter continued to revise the model over several decades and later worked on real-time classroom coaching (not verified: details of his later roles and organisations).

## Core ideas

**Rights.** The teacher has the right to set the limits of the classroom, to expect pupils to follow them and to receive backing from school leaders and parents. Pupils have the right to a teacher who sets firm, consistent limits, gives positive encouragement and tells them clearly what is expected.

**Three response styles.**

- *Non-assertive*: vague, pleading or inconsistent ('Will you please try to settle down?'); threats that are not followed through.
- *Hostile*: shouting, sarcasm, put-downs, sanctions out of proportion. Gets short-term compliance at the cost of relationships.
- *Assertive*: states clearly and calmly what is wanted, and backs words with action, whether recognition or consequence.

**The classroom discipline plan.** Three parts, taught to pupils and shared with parents and leaders:

1. **Rules**: a small number, observable and applying at all times (for example 'Follow directions the first time they are given').
2. **Positive recognition**: praise, positive notes and calls home, privileges and class-wide rewards for those who follow the rules.
3. **Consequences**: a hierarchy of four or five steps for breaking a rule within a day or lesson. Typically: warning; a short penalty such as a minute after class; a longer one; contact with parents; referral to a senior colleague. A 'severe clause' allows immediate removal for serious behaviour. Each lesson or day starts afresh.

**Consequences as choice.** The teacher frames sanctions as the pupil's choice: the rule and consequence were known in advance. This is intended to remove emotion and argument from the exchange.

**Techniques.** Canter popularised several moves now part of general classroom craft:

- the **broken record**: calmly repeating the direction rather than being drawn into debate;
- **positive repetition** (later called behavioural narration): after giving a direction, name pupils who are following it before correcting those who are not;
- specific directions for each activity: what pupils should be doing, at what noise level, with what movement;
- **proximity** and a quiet word at the desk in place of public reprimand.

**Later revisions.** In response to criticism, later editions told teachers to **teach** the plan and specific routines explicitly, to keep recognition well ahead of consequences, to build relationships with the hardest pupils through one-to-one conversations and individual plans, and to stop writing names on the board. Education Week quoted Canter in 1990 saying he no longer advocated that practice.

## Putting it into practice

Few English schools use 'Assertive Discipline' by name now, but its structure is everywhere. To use the sound parts well:

- **Give directions that can be followed.** Before any activity, specify what to do, how much talk and what movement: 'Working on questions 1 to 5, in silence, hands up if you need me.'
- **Narrate before you correct.** 'Aisha has started. Table two are all writing.' Then deal privately with those who have not begun.
- **Use the assertive register.** Say what you want as a statement, not a question or plea. Calm voice, name, direction: 'Liam, I need you facing the front.'
- **Do not argue.** Repeat the direction once or twice (the broken record), then state the choice and move away.
- **Apply the ladder mechanically, not emotionally.** Same steps for every pupil, in order; warn privately where you can; never skip steps in anger or add extras.
- **Keep the ratio positive.** Canter's later guidance was that recognition should far outweigh consequences. Make positive contact home routine.
- **Fresh start.** Do not carry yesterday's tally into today.

**For leaders reviewing a consequence ladder**

- Is it short enough to remember and used the same way in every room?
- Does it include teaching of routines and positive recognition, or only sanctions?
- What happens for pupils who reach the top repeatedly? A ladder alone will not change them; Canter's later work said these pupils need individual plans and relationships.
- Are warnings public (names on boards) or private? Remove public shaming devices.
- Are reasonable adjustments built in for pupils with SEND, as the Equality Act requires?

## Critiques and debate

- **Weak evidence.** Render, Padilla and Krank (Educational Leadership, 1989) reviewed the research and concluded that the effectiveness claims made for the programme were not supported by the small body of studies available. Canter disputed this, saying critics attended to only parts of the model.
- **Obedience, not responsibility.** Curwin and Mendler, and later Alfie Kohn, argued that the approach produces compliance through rewards and punishments without developing pupils' own judgement or addressing why they misbehave. Rethinking Schools published a similar critique from a classroom teacher's perspective.
- **Public shaming.** Names and ticks on the board were criticised (Education Week cites Linda Darling-Hammond) as potentially harmful; Canter dropped the practice.
- **UK debate.** British educational psychologists argued both sides in the 1990s: some described it as a dated, behaviourist import, others reported improved on-task behaviour in schools using it (journal exchange in *Educational Psychology in Practice*, mid-1990s; not opened for this entry).
- **Rigidity and inclusion.** A fixed hierarchy treats all pupils alike. Critics argue this penalises pupils with additional needs; the model's defenders say consistency is what makes it fair.
- **Legacy.** Later writers build on or react against it. Bill Rogers keeps the clarity but adds a less intrusive, more conversational repertoire; Paul Dix rejects escalating ladders in favour of scripts and restorative follow-up; Doug Lemov and Tom Bennett retain consequences but place far more weight on taught routines and culture.
