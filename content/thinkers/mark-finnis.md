## At a glance

- Finnis presents restorative practice as a **way of being**, not a behaviour technique: most of it is proactive relationship-building, with repair after harm as the smaller part.
- His organising model is the **social discipline window**: high challenge plus high support means working **with** people, as opposed to doing things **to** them, **for** them or **not** at all.
- Core tools: affective statements, restorative questions, circles, and 'fair process' when making decisions.
- Restorative does not mean no consequences. It means consequences that involve the pupil in understanding and repairing the harm.
- Evidence is promising but mixed, and implementation is demanding. Poorly resourced versions are the main reason restorative approaches fail in secondary schools.

## Who they are

Mark Finnis is a UK trainer and consultant and the director of **L30 Relational Systems**. His publisher describes him as an Independent Thinking Associate and one of the UK's leading exponents of restorative practice. He has worked for many years with schools, local authorities, children's social care and other public services, including work connected with Hull's programme to become a 'restorative city' (details not verified against a primary source). His work draws on the restorative practices tradition developed by Ted Wachtel and colleagues at the International Institute for Restorative Practices (IIRP), and he is closely aligned with Paul Dix's relational approach.

## Core ideas

**Relationships first.** Finnis argues that schools run on relationships and that these should be built by design, not left to chance. A recurring image is the relational 'bank account': every small positive interaction is a deposit, and you can only make a withdrawal (a challenge, a correction) from an account that is in credit. His slogan is to connect before you correct, and to put connection before content.

**Mostly proactive.** He suggests that around 80 per cent of restorative practice is proactive (building community, teaching social skills, creating belonging) and 20 per cent responsive (dealing with conflict and harm). Schools that adopt only the responsive part, the 'restorative conversation' after an incident, have misunderstood the approach.

**The social discipline window.** Adapted from McCold and Wachtel. Two axes, challenge (limits, expectations, boundaries) and support (encouragement, nurture), give four ways of exercising authority:

| | Low support | High support |
|---|---|---|
| **High challenge** | TO: punitive, authoritarian | WITH: restorative, authoritative |
| **Low challenge** | NOT: neglectful | FOR: permissive, rescuing |

Finnis's point is that restorative practice is the high-challenge, high-support quadrant. It is not the soft option; permissiveness is a different box.

**A continuum of practice.** From informal to formal: affective statements; affective or restorative questions; small impromptu conversations; circles; formal restorative conferences. The informal end is used many times a day by every adult; formal conferences are rare and need a trained facilitator.

**Affective language.** Adults say how behaviour affects them and others, separating the deed from the doer: 'I was frustrated when the talking carried on, because others couldn't hear.' Praise is equally specific.

**Restorative questions.** A consistent sequence used with everyone involved: What happened? What were you thinking and feeling at the time? Who has been affected, and how? What is needed to put things right? What will we do differently next time? Finnis advises against 'why did you do it?', which invites defensiveness.

**Circles.** Pupils and adult sit in a circle; a talking piece passes round; everyone may speak or pass. Used for check-in and check-out in tutor time, for building community, for discussing learning and, when needed, for solving problems.

**Fair process.** Drawing on Kim and Mauborgne's work, people accept decisions, even unwelcome ones, when they have been **engaged**, given an **explanation** and are **clear about expectations**. Finnis applies this to staff as well as pupils: culture change imposed on staff is a 'to', not a 'with'.

**Culture by design.** Every school has a culture; the question is whether it is deliberate. He stresses that the small daily habits (greetings, names, tone, how adults speak about pupils) are the culture.

## Putting it into practice

**Teachers and tutors**

- Greet every pupil by name at the door. Finnis's test: are pupils noticed when they are there and missed when they are not?
- Run a two-minute check-in circle in tutor time once or twice a week: one low-risk prompt ('One thing you're looking forward to this week'), talking piece, right to pass.
- Use affective statements for both praise and correction in place of generic labels.
- After an incident, once everyone is calm, use two or three restorative questions. Agree one concrete action. Check back a few days later.
- Keep the challenge high: restate the expectation plainly and hold the pupil to the agreed action.

**Leaders**

- Decide what is non-negotiable (the challenge) and what support exists, and communicate both. Restorative practice sits alongside clear rules; DfE guidance still expects a behaviour policy with sanctions.
- Train all staff, including support staff, in the language and questions; train a smaller group to facilitate formal conferences.
- Timetable the capacity: who covers a class so a restorative conversation can happen the same day?
- Use circles and fair process in staff meetings and line management. Staff who are done 'to' will not work 'with' pupils.
- Give it time. Advocates describe whole-school implementation as a multi-year culture change, not a policy rewrite.
- Monitor: suspensions, removals, repeat incidents, pupil and staff voice. Be honest if low-level disruption rises.

## Critiques and debate

- **Evidence.** The EEF's *Improving Behaviour in Schools* guidance describes the evidence for restorative approaches as limited. The largest English trial of a whole-school programme including restorative practice (the 'Learning Together' study, Bonell and colleagues, 2018) reported reduced bullying; a US randomised study in Pittsburgh (RAND, 2018) found fewer suspensions and better teacher-rated climate but no academic gain and worse attainment in middle-school grades. Neither study was opened for this entry; treat these summaries as indicative.
- **Consequence-led critics.** Tom Bennett and others argue that restorative conversations, used in place of sanctions, can leave victims and teachers unsupported, be gamed by pupils, and consume staff time. They accept restorative conversations as a supplement to consequences, not a replacement.
- **Implementation risk.** Researchers and practitioners agree that partial adoption is common: a script of questions bolted on to an unchanged system, or sanctions removed with nothing built in their place. Finnis makes the same criticism.
- **Pressure on those harmed.** Critics caution that pupils (and staff) who have been harmed may feel obliged to take part in a meeting with the person responsible. Good practice makes participation voluntary and never uses conferences for bullying or abuse without specialist judgement and safeguarding advice.
- **Time and workload.** Done properly it is labour-intensive. Schools need to weigh that against the time currently spent on repeat sanctions.
- **Common ground.** Both sides endorse calm adults, explicit expectations and strong relationships; the dispute is about what should happen after rules are broken, and in what proportion.
