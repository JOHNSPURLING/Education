## At a glance

- Myatt's leadership maxim is **high challenge, low threat**: people do their best work when the demand is high and the fear is low.
- On curriculum, her test is **coherence**: does this unit connect to what came before and after, and to the big ideas of the subject, or is it a 'gallimaufry' (a jumble) of activities?
- She argues for **concepts** as the organising structure, **stories and rich texts** as the vehicle, and **fewer things in greater depth**.
- She is critical of busywork: worksheets, gap-fills and over-scaffolded tasks that produce completed pages without thinking.
- The **Huh** books (with John Tomsett) model how senior leaders should talk with subject leaders about curriculum.
- Her work is practical and humane in tone, synthesising research for busy teachers; it is not original research and should be read alongside subject-specific sources.

## Who they are

Mary Myatt is an education adviser, writer and speaker. She has taught in secondary schools (her background is in religious education), worked as a local authority adviser and inspected schools. She now writes, speaks and curates **Myatt & Co**, an online platform of short professional development films and subject networks for primary and secondary teachers. She was closely involved in the curriculum conversation that surrounded Ofsted's 2019 framework, and her books are among the most widely read by curriculum and subject leaders in England.

## Core ideas

**High challenge, low threat.** In her 2016 leadership book Myatt argues that humans are a 'challenge-seeking species' but only when the stakes for getting it wrong are low. Leaders should therefore set demanding work and have candid conversations, while removing unnecessary threat: no graded lesson observations, feedback as conversation not judgement, and an assumption of good intent. Her shorthand is that staff are humans first and professionals second. The same principle applies to pupils: hard material, safe classroom.

**From gallimaufry to coherence.** A curriculum is coherent when pupils can see how new material connects to what they already know and to the subject's big ideas. Myatt asks planners to check each unit for its place in the larger story of the subject, and to decide deliberately what to leave out. A Schools Week review summarised her position as planning being as much about what not to teach as what to include.

**Concepts as 'holding baskets'.** Facts are easier to learn and retain when attached to concepts (for example 'empire', 'adaptation', 'tragedy', 'proportion'). Concepts recur across units and years, so identifying and revisiting them is what turns a list of topics into a curriculum. Teaching the vocabulary and etymology of key terms is a routine she recommends for every subject.

**Curriculum as narrative.** Drawing on cognitive psychology (notably Daniel Willingham's argument that stories are easier to remember than other material), Myatt argues that units should have narrative shape and that pupils should meet **high-quality texts and stories** in every subject, including full texts, primary sources and demanding non-fiction, not only extracts and slides.

**Challenge for all.** She is sceptical of differentiating by giving some pupils easier tasks. The better route is the same ambitious content with scaffolding, talk and support for those who need it, and with the scaffolds removed over time. She warns against confusing task completion with learning: neat, finished worksheets are a poor proxy.

**Fewer things, greater depth.** *Back on Track* (2020) argues for stripping out low-value activity (excessive marking, data drops, generic lesson requirements) so that time goes to curriculum thinking and subject knowledge.

**Curriculum conversations.** The **Huh** series (the title refers to an Egyptian deity associated with endlessness, signalling that curriculum work is never finished; this gloss comes from the authors and is not independently verified here) presents conversations between senior and subject leaders. The model: the line manager asks genuine questions about what is taught, why, in what order and how the subject leader knows it is working, and learns about the subject rather than checking compliance.

## Putting it into practice

**Teachers and subject leaders**

- For the next unit, write down the three or four concepts it is really about. Check where pupils met each before and where they will meet it again. If nowhere, the unit is an island.
- Open with a story, a rich source or a demanding text. Read it aloud, with the class following, and teach the key vocabulary, including where the words come from.
- Audit one week of tasks. Remove anything pupils can complete without thinking about the content (word searches, colouring, copying, gap-fills with a word bank that does the work).
- Keep the destination the same for everyone. Plan scaffolds for pupils who need them and plan when you will take them away.
- Ask pupils to produce fewer, better pieces of work, redrafted to a high standard.

**Senior leaders**

- Run a Huh-style conversation with each subject lead. Useful questions, paraphrased from the approach: What are the big ideas in your subject? Why this content, in this order? Talk me through one unit: what should pupils know and be able to do by the end? What do pupils find hard? What are you reading, and which subject association are you in? What do you need from me?
- Look at pupils' work and talk to pupils with the subject lead, as shared enquiry, not inspection.
- Protect time for subject teams to work on curriculum, and cut a low-value demand to pay for it.
- Apply 'high challenge, low threat' to feedback: say the difficult thing plainly, kindly and privately, and assume the colleague wants to improve.

## Critiques and debate

- **Synthesis, not research.** Myatt's books are short, accessible digests of other people's research and of her own experience. That is their value, but claims (for example about narrative and memory) should be traced to the underlying sources before being built into policy.
- **Breadth versus depth.** The Schools Week reviewer of *The Curriculum* praised it as timely and thought-provoking but doubted whether a few pages per subject would be enough for actual planning. Subject-specific guidance from subject associations and the Ofsted research reviews is needed alongside.
- **Generic principles and subject difference.** Curriculum specialists such as Christine Counsell stress that 'coherence', 'concepts' and 'narrative' mean different things in different disciplines. Story works differently in history than in mathematics; treating it as a universal template risks distortion.
- **Low threat and accountability.** Some leaders question whether removing graded observation and reducing monitoring is realistic in schools under pressure. Myatt's reply is that low threat is not low standards, and that fear produces compliance, not improvement.
- **Association with Ofsted's 2019 framework.** Critics of the 'curriculum turn', including some unions and primary leaders, argued it generated workload and a new orthodoxy of curriculum paperwork. Myatt has herself warned against producing intent statements and documents for inspectors rather than for pupils.
