## At a glance

- Dix's central claim: pupil behaviour changes when **every adult behaves consistently**, so start with staff habits, not a longer sanctions ladder.
- His framework is **five pillars**: consistent, calm adult behaviour; first attention to best conduct; relentless routines; scripting difficult interventions; restorative follow-up.
- Signature tools: **three simple rules** (commonly 'Ready, Respectful, Safe'), **meet and greet**, **recognition boards**, the **30-second scripted intervention** and the **restorative conversation**.
- He does not say 'no consequences'. He argues consequences should be small, certain, proportionate and followed by repair, and that heavy punishment and public shaming do not change the pupils who receive them most.
- Widely adopted in English schools, and widely argued over: critics say it under-weights sanctions and loads responsibility onto teachers. Read alongside Tom Bennett and the DfE's *Behaviour in Schools* guidance.

## Who they are

Paul Dix is a former teacher who became a behaviour trainer and founded Pivotal Education, a UK training company. He has worked extensively with schools, alternative provision, pupil referral units and colleges, and his reputation rests on work in settings where behaviour is hardest. He led the 'Ban the Booths' campaign against the routine use of isolation booths (launched around 2018; date not verified against a primary source). His 2017 book became one of the best-selling UK education titles, and many secondary behaviour policies borrow its vocabulary. Not verified: his current company arrangements as of 2026; Pivotal Education has changed ownership since he founded it.

## Core ideas

**The five pillars** (the order is given differently in different summaries; this is the list as reported in reviews of the 2017 book):

1. **Consistent, calm adult behaviour.** Adults do not shout, show anger or take behaviour personally. Emotional restraint is treated as a professional skill, not a personality trait. Consistency means the whole staff doing a small number of agreed things, rather than each teacher running a private system.
2. **First attention to best conduct.** Notice and acknowledge pupils doing the right thing before dealing with those who are not. The pupils who get the teacher's attention first set the tone of the room.
3. **Relentless routines.** A few routines, taught and insisted on by everyone until automatic: entry, transitions, getting attention, exit.
4. **Scripting difficult interventions.** Staff use the same short, rehearsed form of words when behaviour goes wrong, so the adult stays calm and the pupil hears the same message from everyone.
5. **Restorative follow-up.** After an incident, the adult who was there has a structured conversation with the pupil to repair the relationship and plan for next time.

**Three simple rules.** Dix argues long rule lists are unlearnable. He recommends three, known by every pupil and adult; 'Ready, Respectful, Safe' is the set most schools adopt. All conversations about behaviour refer back to them.

**Deliberate botheredness.** Small, persistent, daily acts that show pupils the adult cares: greeting by name, remembering details, following up, a note or call home for good conduct. He frames this as the engine of relationships, not an add-on.

**Recognition over reward.** He is sceptical of token economies and prizes. He prefers a **recognition board**: one target behaviour for the lesson written on the board (for example 'one voice'), names added as pupils show it, aiming for the whole class. Names never come off. He also advocates positive notes and calls home, and leader recognition such as 'Hot Chocolate Friday'.

**Stepped responses.** A typical Dix-style sequence is: reminder of the rule; caution delivered privately; last chance, using the 30-second script; time out (a short spell outside the room or in another class); repair. Each step is quiet and private where possible.

**On sanctions.** Dix argues that escalating punishment has diminishing returns, that the same pupils fill detentions and isolation week after week, and that certainty and follow-up matter more than severity. He is critical of 'behaviour points' systems, names on the board for misbehaviour, and prolonged isolation. He accepts that consequences are needed, but wants them proportionate and paired with a restorative conversation. *After the Adults Change* (2021) extends this to whole-school relational practice and reducing what the publisher describes as disproportionate punishment.

## Putting it into practice

**Classroom teacher, Monday morning**

- Stand at the door. Greet each pupil by name; set the first task before they sit.
- Put one recognition target on the board. Add names as you see it. Finish the lesson by acknowledging the class total.
- Rehearse a 30-second script and use it word for word. Dix's structure, paraphrased: say what you have noticed; name the rule it breaks; state the consequence the pupil has chosen; remind them of a recent time they got it right and say that is the pupil you expect to see; thank them and walk away. Do not wait for a reply or get drawn into argument: give take-up time.
- Deal with secondary behaviours (muttering, eye-rolling) later, not in the moment.
- Make one positive phone call or send one positive note home each day.

**The restorative conversation.** Held by the adult involved, briefly, usually the same day. Choose a few questions from a short list: What happened? What were you thinking at the time? Who has been affected, and how? What needs to happen to put things right? What will you do differently next time? Dix advises picking two or three, not working through all of them, and walking or sitting side by side rather than across a desk.

**Leaders**

- Agree three rules and three or four 'visible consistencies' (for example meet and greet, a single attention signal, calm corridors) and hold staff to them. Dix's advice is to do less, but ensure everyone does it.
- Write shared scripts into the policy and practise them in staff training.
- Be visible at transitions; leaders model the calm tone they ask for.
- Audit who receives repeated sanctions; if the same pupils dominate, the sanction is not working for them.
- Protect time for restorative conversations: they fail when staff have no slot to hold them.

## Critiques and debate

- **Under-weighting consequences.** Advocates of consequence-led systems, including Tom Bennett, argue that schools need clear, reliably applied sanctions alongside relationships, and that removing or softening them can leave teachers unsupported and classrooms disrupted. Some teachers and bloggers (for example Andrew Old) have reported that 'Dix-style' policies, poorly implemented, became sanction-free in practice.
- **Blaming teachers.** The book's title is read by some as implying poor behaviour is the adult's fault. Dix's reply is that adult behaviour is the variable schools control.
- **Evidence base.** Dix's work draws on practitioner experience and case studies, not controlled trials. The EEF's *Improving Behaviour in Schools* guidance supports several compatible ideas (knowing pupils, teaching routines, consistency) but describes the evidence for whole-school restorative approaches in England as limited.
- **Isolation.** His opposition to booths is contested: DfE guidance treats removal from the classroom as a legitimate sanction when used properly, and many heads argue it protects other pupils' learning.
- **Workload.** Restorative conversations and daily positive contact take time. Without timetabled capacity they are the first things to slip.
- **Common ground.** Dix, Rogers and Bennett agree on more than the debate suggests: taught routines, calm adults, certainty over severity, and whole-staff consistency.
