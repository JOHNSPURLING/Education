## At a glance

- Performance during a lesson is an unreliable guide to learning. Conditions that produce quick, smooth performance often produce poor long-term retention, and the reverse.
- 'Desirable difficulties' are challenges that slow apparent progress but strengthen memory: spacing, interleaving, retrieval, generation and variation.
- A difficulty is desirable only if the learner can overcome it. Without the necessary prior knowledge it is just a difficulty.
- Memories have two strengths: storage strength (how well learned) and retrieval strength (how accessible right now). Some forgetting makes relearning more powerful.
- Pupils, and teachers, misjudge what works. Rereading and massed practice feel effective because they feel fluent.

## Who they are

Robert A. Bjork is distinguished research professor of psychology at the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA). Elizabeth Ligon Bjork is professor of psychology at UCLA. They have collaborated for decades and jointly run the Bjork Learning and Forgetting Lab. Both have held senior roles in American psychological science; Robert Bjork is a past president of the Association for Psychological Science and chaired a US National Research Council committee on techniques for enhancing human performance. Their former students and collaborators, such as Nate Kornell, include many of the researchers behind the modern literature on spacing, interleaving and metacognition.

## Core ideas

**Learning versus performance.** Learning is a relatively permanent change in knowledge or understanding that supports long-term retention and transfer. Performance is what can be observed during or immediately after instruction. Soderstrom and Robert Bjork's 2015 review collected evidence from motor and verbal learning that the two can dissociate: substantial learning can occur with no visible improvement, and impressive performance in the lesson can leave little lasting trace. The implication is that 'they could all do it by the end of the lesson' proves little.

**The new theory of disuse (1992).** Each memory has:

- *Storage strength*: how thoroughly it is embedded and connected with other knowledge. It accumulates and, in the theory, is not lost.
- *Retrieval strength*: how easily it can be accessed now. It rises with recent study and decays with disuse and interference.

Key predictions: retrieval capacity is limited, storage is not. The lower the current retrieval strength, the bigger the gain in storage strength from a successful retrieval or restudy. So allowing some forgetting, and then retrieving with effort, builds more durable memory than revisiting material while it is still fresh. Forgetting, on this view, is a helpful feature of memory, not just a failure.

**Desirable difficulties.** Robert Bjork introduced the term in 1994. The main ones:

- *Spacing*: distributing study or practice over time instead of massing it.
- *Interleaving*: mixing topics or problem types instead of blocking them. Kornell and Bjork (2008) found that interleaving paintings by different artists improved learning of each artist's style, even though most participants believed blocking had worked better.
- *Retrieval practice (testing)*: using tests as learning events, not only as assessments.
- *Generation*: producing an answer or solution instead of being shown it. This includes pretesting: attempting questions before being taught can improve later learning of the material even when the attempts are wrong.
- *Varying the conditions of practice*: changing context, format or examples instead of keeping practice constant and predictable.
- *Reducing or delaying feedback* in some circumstances, so that learners do not become dependent on it.

**The qualifier.** The word 'desirable' matters. A difficulty helps only when it triggers processing that supports learning and when the learner has the background knowledge to respond successfully. Elizabeth and Robert Bjork have repeatedly stressed that not all difficulties are desirable.

**Metacognitive illusions.** Learners judge learning by fluency. Rereading notes, highlighting and cramming create a sense of familiarity that is mistaken for knowledge. With Dunlosky and Kornell (2013), Robert Bjork argued that becoming an effective learner means understanding these illusions and choosing harder strategies.

**Elizabeth Bjork's contributions.** With Michael Anderson and Robert Bjork she established retrieval-induced forgetting: retrieving some items can reduce access to related, unretrieved items. With Jeri Little and others she showed that well-built multiple-choice questions, with plausible competing options, can strengthen memory for the rejected alternatives as well as the correct answer.

## Putting it into practice

For a classroom teacher:

- **Quiz on last week, last month and last term**, not only last lesson. Expect lower scores and tell pupils why that is useful.
- **Mix practice once the basics are secure.** Block when first teaching a method; then set mixed exercises in which pupils must decide which method applies.
- **Leave gaps before returning.** Revisit a topic after pupils have begun to forget it, and build this into homework and starters.
- **Ask before you tell.** A short pre-question or prediction before an explanation costs little and can prime learning.
- **Write multiple-choice questions with plausible distractors** so that pupils have to think about why each is wrong.
- **Teach pupils the science.** Explain that self-testing and spaced revision feel worse than rereading but work better. Do this explicitly in Years 10 to 13.

For leaders:

- Be cautious about judging learning from a single lesson observation or from pupils' fluency in the lesson. Look at retention over time.
- Design schemes of work and homework to revisit content at intervals, and assessments to be cumulative.
- Make sure retrieval practice is low-stakes and does not become a daily ritual of the same easy questions.

## Critiques and debate

- **Laboratory evidence.** Much of the research uses university students, word pairs or short texts, and retention intervals of days. Classroom studies exist, particularly in mathematics for interleaving and spacing, but are fewer, and effects in school trials tend to be smaller than in the lab.
- **Tension with cognitive load theory.** John Sweller's work recommends reducing load for novices; the Bjorks recommend adding certain difficulties. Researchers on both sides have suggested that the two apply to different situations: reduce load when material is complex and new, add difficulty when it is within reach. Where exactly the boundary lies is unsettled.
- **Interleaving has limits.** Reviews (for example Brunmair and Richter, 2019) find that benefits depend on the material: strongest when categories are easily confused, weak or negative for some verbal materials and for learners with low prior knowledge.
- **Pretesting and generation with novices.** Errors generated without enough knowledge may waste time or demotivate; evidence that such errors are harmless comes mostly from settings with prompt feedback.
- **Motivation.** Pupils who experience difficulty as failure may disengage. Teachers need to manage success rates and explain the purpose, a point the Bjorks acknowledge.
- **Implementation drift.** In English schools, 'retrieval practice' has sometimes become a compliance routine of factual starter quizzes; this is a criticism of the application, made by writers such as Kate Jones and Tom Sherrington, not of the underlying research.
