## At a glance

- Strickland writes as a serving head: his books are **how-to manuals**, not theory.
- Central claim: **behaviour is a leadership responsibility**. Senior leaders must own the system, be visible and take sanctions off teachers' shoulders.
- He favours **centralised systems** (one set of rules, centrally run detentions, an on-call and removal system) so that teachers can teach.
- Routines are taught explicitly and rehearsed; expectations are 'warm-strict'; small things are attended to.
- *The Behaviour Manual* offers over 100 short strategies organised by role; *They Don't Behave for Me* works through 50 classroom scenarios.
- His position sits with Tom Bennett's on the consequence-and-culture side of the behaviour debate.

## Core ideas

**Leaders own behaviour.** Strickland argues that when behaviour is poor, the cause is usually systemic: unclear expectations, inconsistent follow-through and invisible leaders. Teachers should not be left to run private systems or to chase their own detentions. Leaders set the standard, are present on corridors and at the gate, and take responsibility for serious and persistent issues.

**Clarity and simplicity.** A behaviour policy should be short enough to be known by every pupil, parent and member of staff. Expectations are concrete (what to bring, how to enter, how to sit, how to move around the building), and the response to a breach is the same in every classroom.

**Centralisation.** One consequence ladder used by all. Detentions set by teachers but run centrally by leaders. A staffed on-call system that responds quickly. A removal room with clear entry and exit expectations. The argument is workload and fairness: consistency is impossible if each teacher must enforce alone.

**Routines and rehearsal.** Entry and exit routines, transitions, equipment checks, lining up and a common attention signal are taught at the start of the year and re-taught. Staff rehearse them in training. He treats induction of new staff and new pupils as part of the behaviour system.

**Warm-strict.** High expectations are held together with visible care. Strickland stresses greeting pupils, knowing names, praise and recognition, and talking with pupils after a sanction. He rejects the idea that strict systems and good relationships are opposites.

**Roles at every level.** *The Behaviour Manual* is organised around who does what: senior leaders, middle and pastoral leaders, classroom teachers, tutors, early career teachers and support staff each have a section of one-page strategies. Reviewers describe it as a what-to-do book rather than a why-to-do book.

**Teaching and curriculum.** The *Education Exposed* books set behaviour within a wider model: a knowledge-rich curriculum, teacher-led explicit instruction, limited and purposeful assessment, and reduced marking and data workload. Behaviour is the precondition; curriculum and teaching are the purpose.

**Scenario thinking.** *They Don't Behave for Me* takes 50 common situations, from low-level chatter to refusal and confrontation, and sets out possible responses, on the principle that teachers handle incidents better when they have thought them through in advance.

## Putting it into practice

**Senior leaders**

- Write the non-negotiables on one page. Test whether a Year 7 pupil and a supply teacher could both recite them.
- Publish a duty map and stick to it: leaders at the gate, in corridors at every changeover, in the canteen. Visibility is the cheapest intervention available.
- Centralise detentions. Teachers log; leaders run, chase and escalate non-attendance.
- Staff an on-call rota with a response-time expectation, and review the data weekly: which lessons, which pupils, which times of day.
- Script the removal process: what the teacher says, where the pupil goes, what work they do, who speaks to them and how they return to the next lesson.
- Start each term with a re-induction for pupils and a rehearsal session for staff. Give new and supply staff a short briefing before their first lesson.
- Walk the building asking one question: is the standard the same in every room? Where it is not, coach, do not blame.

**Classroom teachers**

- Use the school's routines exactly as written, especially when you would prefer your own; pupils benefit from sameness across the day.
- Meet pupils at the door, have work ready, and narrate the positive before correcting.
- Follow the consequence steps in order, calmly and without debate; log accurately so leaders can act.
- Take one scenario you find hard (a pupil refusing to move seats, for example) and plan your first three sentences.
- After any removal, have a brief reset conversation before the next lesson.

**Middle and pastoral leaders**

- Use behaviour data to spot patterns by class, subject and time. Support the teacher first.
- Hold the line on routines within your team and model them in your own classroom.

## Critiques and debate

- **Practitioner evidence.** Strickland's authority rests on leading one school's improvement. Schools Week's review of *The Behaviour Manual* saw that as the book's selling point, and also noted that it is practical rather than theoretical: it tells readers what to do more than why. Transfer to different contexts is argued, not demonstrated.
- **Centralised, consequence-led systems.** Relational and restorative practitioners (Paul Dix, Mark Finnis) argue that ladders, centralised detentions and removal rooms manage behaviour without changing it, and that the same pupils cycle through. Strickland and others reply that calm classrooms are the foundation for relationships and that most pupils benefit from predictability.
- **Inclusion.** Critics of strict systems argue that they disadvantage pupils with SEND or unmet needs and can raise suspensions. Proponents argue for adjustments within a consistent framework. The DfE's *Behaviour in Schools* guidance expects both clear systems and reasonable adjustments.
- **Teacher autonomy.** Some teachers find highly prescribed routines deskilling. Supporters argue that shared routines free attention for teaching and protect new staff.
- **Breadth.** The books range across behaviour, curriculum, teaching and leadership in short sections; readers wanting depth on any one area will need other sources.
