## At a glance

- Bennett's core claim: good behaviour is not a natural default. It has to be **taught, practised and maintained**, like any other curriculum.
- Teachers 'run the room' through **norms, routines and consequences**; leaders create the culture that makes this possible in every room.
- His 2017 DfE review, *Creating a Culture*, found that **how a school is led** shapes behaviour more than the skill of individual teachers working alone.
- He is proactive first: most of the work is prevention, teaching and relationship-building, with sanctions as a necessary backstop, not the main tool.
- He has shaped national policy: the 2016 ITT behaviour review, the Behaviour Hubs programme and the direction of DfE behaviour guidance.
- Contested points: removal rooms, suspensions, mobile phone bans and how far 'high expectations' can flex for pupils with additional needs.

## Who they are

Tom Bennett taught in London secondary schools for over a decade (one profile says 14 years), mainly religious studies and philosophy, and wrote a long-running behaviour advice column for Tes. In 2013 he founded **researchED**, a teacher-led conference movement promoting research-informed practice, now running events internationally. In 2015 he was appointed to advise the Department for Education on behaviour. He chaired the group that produced the 2016 report on behaviour content in initial teacher training, wrote the 2017 independent review *Creating a Culture*, and led the DfE-funded **Behaviour Hubs** programme, which one 2025 profile says supported over 650 schools before closing. That profile describes him as the DfE's independent behaviour adviser; his own Substack describes him as a professor of school behaviour at Academica University, Amsterdam. Not verified: his exact DfE title and status as of November 2026.

## Core ideas

**Behaviour is a curriculum.** Pupils arrive with very different experience of what school behaviour looks like. Assuming they know how to behave advantages those who have already been taught at home. So schools should define the behaviour they want in concrete terms, teach it explicitly, model it, practise it, check it and re-teach it. This is the origin of the 'behaviour curriculum' language now found in DfE guidance.

**Norms.** People do what they think everyone else is doing. The teacher's job is to make the desired behaviour the visible, expected norm of the room: state it, notice it, narrate it, and act when it is breached so that pupils see the norm is real.

**Routines.** Routines are the building blocks of classroom culture: entering, starting work, distributing materials, asking questions, transitions, leaving. Each is designed in detail, taught step by step and practised until automatic. Good routines save time, cut decisions and make the room feel safe and predictable.

**Consequences.** Bennett treats consequences as broader than sanctions: they include praise, recognition and reward as well as penalties. Sanctions should be certain, proportionate and predictable; severity matters less than reliability. They are a signal that boundaries are real, not a cure in themselves.

**Proactive before reactive.** *Running the Room* uses a firefighting analogy: fire prevention does more than firefighting. Most effort should go into designing the environment, teaching expectations and building relationships; the remainder is responding well when things still go wrong, including scripted responses and, where needed, removal from the lesson.

**Relationships, properly understood.** He argues relationships are built on structure, competence, high expectations and reliability, not on being liked, and that they are an outcome of a well-run room as much as a precondition.

**Creating a Culture (2017).** The review visited schools with strong behaviour and found common features:

- highly visible leaders with ambitious, detailed visions of conduct;
- a clear, shared understanding of 'how we do things here';
- expectations set as high as possible for everyone, with support to meet them;
- attention to detail and to routines in corridors, canteens and public spaces, not only classrooms;
- consistent practice by all staff, sustained by induction and training;
- commitment from staff and parents to the school's approach.

Its summary for leaders was to **design the culture deliberately, build it in detail, and maintain it constantly**. It found no single fix, and it recommended better leadership training in behaviour and funding for internal inclusion units.

## Putting it into practice

**Teachers**

- List the routines your lessons depend on. For each, write the steps, teach them explicitly ('This is how we enter: line up here, silent from the door, equipment out, start the task on the board'), practise, and give feedback as you would on written work.
- Re-teach after every holiday and whenever standards drift. Bennett's point is that drift is normal and maintenance is the job.
- Make norms visible: 'Almost everyone has started. Thank you, back row.' Correct small breaches early and calmly.
- Decide your responses in advance. Know the first, second and third thing you will say and do, so that you are not improvising under pressure.
- Follow up every consequence you set. If you cannot follow it up, do not set it.
- Do not wait for behaviour to go wrong before teaching it; invest heavily in the first lessons with any class.

**Leaders**

- Write down, concretely, what good conduct looks like in each part of the school day: arrival, corridors, lunch, lessons, departure.
- Teach it to pupils in a planned induction at the start of the year and for mid-year arrivals; train every adult, including support and supply staff.
- Be visible. Leaders at the gate, in corridors and dropping into lessons is a recurring feature of the review's case-study schools.
- Remove the burden of consequences from individual teachers where possible: centralised detentions, clear on-call and removal systems.
- Audit consistency: do pupils experience the same expectations in every room?
- Plan support for pupils who struggle to meet expectations. The review argues for well-resourced internal units with a route back to class.

## Critiques and debate

- **Relational and restorative critics.** Practitioners such as Paul Dix and advocates of trauma-informed practice argue that consequence-backed systems rely too heavily on sanctions and removal, and can damage relationships with the pupils who most need them. Bennett's reply is that structure and relationships are complementary, and that disruption harms the most disadvantaged pupils most.
- **Inclusion and SEND.** Some SEND organisations and campaigners argue that uniform high expectations and removal rooms fall hardest on pupils with additional needs and contribute to rising suspensions. Bennett argues for reasonable adjustments within consistent expectations, not lower expectations.
- **Evidence.** *Creating a Culture* was based on school visits and interviews, not experimental evidence, and the report acknowledges this. The 'behaviour curriculum' idea is plausible and consistent with EEF guidance on teaching routines, but has not been tested in large trials.
- **Depth and tone.** A Schools Week review of *Running the Room* found it directly usable in classrooms but felt it did not fully explore the thornier questions; another review noted occasional partisan commentary.
- **Policy influence.** Commentators including Education Uncovered have questioned the closeness of researchED and its founder to government. Supporters point to researchED's role in giving teachers direct access to research.
