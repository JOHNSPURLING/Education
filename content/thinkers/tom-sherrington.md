## At a glance

- Tom Sherrington is a former physics teacher and headteacher who now works as a consultant and writes the teacherhead.com blog.
- His main contribution is translation: turning research (Rosenshine, Wiliam, cognitive science) into classroom routines that ordinary teachers can use.
- *Rosenshine's Principles in Action* regroups the ten principles into four strands: sequencing concepts and modelling, questioning, reviewing material, and stages of practice.
- *Teaching WalkThrus* (with Oliver Caviglioli) breaks techniques into five illustrated steps and pairs them with the ADAPT cycle for coaching.
- Recurring themes: 'teach to the top', a knowledge-rich curriculum, checking the understanding of every pupil, and a balance of 'Mode A' and 'Mode B' teaching.

## Who they are

Sherrington taught physics and maths and held leadership posts in several English secondary schools. He was headteacher of King Edward VI Grammar School, Chelmsford, and then of Highbury Grove School in Islington, leaving headship in 2017 (dates from the author's own published biographies; exact years not re-verified for this entry). Since then he has worked as an education consultant (Teacherhead Consulting) with schools and trusts in the UK and internationally, and is a regular speaker at researchED and similar events. His blog, teacherhead.com, has run since 2012 and is one of the most widely read teaching blogs in England.

## Core ideas

**The Learning Rainforest.** The 2017 book uses a tree metaphor for great teaching in three parts:

- *Establishing the conditions* (the roots): attitudes and habits, relationships and behaviour, high expectations, and planning the curriculum.
- *Building the knowledge structure* (the trunk): explaining, modelling, questioning, feedback and practice, so that pupils secure knowledge.
- *Exploring the possibilities* (the canopy): more open-ended work such as projects, extended enquiry, oracy and independent study.

The contrast he draws is between a 'plantation' (uniform, compliance-driven schooling) and a 'managed rainforest' (strong structures with room for diversity).

**Mode A and Mode B.** Introduced in *The Learning Rainforest* and developed on the blog, this is a way of describing balance. Mode A is the mainstay: teacher-led instruction (explain, model, question, practise, give feedback), which he suggests should make up roughly 80% of lessons. Mode B is the remaining slice: enquiry, extended projects, debate, hands-on and creative tasks. His argument is that Mode B enriches the enacted curriculum but has to be planned deliberately and rests on knowledge built in Mode A.

**Rosenshine's four strands.** In *Rosenshine's Principles in Action* (2019) he argues that the ten principles are easier to use when grouped:

1. *Sequencing concepts and modelling*: small steps, models, scaffolds.
2. *Questioning*: asking many questions and checking the understanding of all pupils.
3. *Reviewing material*: daily, weekly and monthly review.
4. *Stages of practice*: guided practice, a high success rate, then independent practice.

He stresses that the principles are not a lesson plan or checklist, and that they apply differently across subjects.

**Teach to the top.** Pitch lessons at the highest attainers in the room and scaffold others up to that level, rather than aiming at the middle and bolting on extension tasks.

**Questioning and checking for understanding.** A staple of his blog and training is a small repertoire of techniques: cold calling, think-pair-share, mini-whiteboards ('show-me boards'), 'say it again better', probing questions and process questions. The underlying point is that a teacher should know what *all* pupils think, not just volunteers.

**Teaching WalkThrus and ADAPT.** With Oliver Caviglioli he produced three volumes of five-step visual guides covering behaviour and relationships, curriculum planning, explaining and modelling, questioning and feedback, practice and retrieval, and Mode B teaching. The guides are designed for instructional coaching. Teachers work through the ADAPT cycle: Attempt the technique, Develop it, Adapt it to the subject and class, Practise, then Test whether it is working. The intent is that WalkThrus are a shared reference point, not a script.

## Putting it into practice

For a classroom teacher:

- **Pick one questioning routine and make it the default.** For example, pose the question, give thinking time, then cold call three pupils before commenting. Or ask every pupil to write an answer on a mini-whiteboard and scan the room before moving on.
- **Use 'say it again better'.** Accept a first, partial answer, then ask the same pupil to restate it in a full sentence with the technical vocabulary.
- **Plan lessons through the four strands.** Ask: what are the small steps and what will I model? Which questions will tell me whether everyone has it? What am I reviewing from last week or last term? How will guided practice hand over to independent practice?
- **Pitch high.** Plan the most demanding version of the task first, then decide the scaffolds.

For leaders:

- Agree a small number of WalkThrus (or equivalent step-by-step descriptions) as shared language for a department or school, and use the ADAPT cycle in coaching so teachers adapt them to their subject.
- Avoid turning Rosenshine or WalkThrus into an observation tick-list. Sherrington has written repeatedly against this.
- Protect time for subject teams to plan some Mode B experiences across a key stage (a project, a debate, an extended practical) so they are not left to chance.

## Critiques and debate

- **Checklist risk.** The popularity of *Rosenshine's Principles in Action* contributed to schools using the principles as a generic lesson template or monitoring form. Sherrington himself criticises this, but critics argue that simplified, branded summaries invite it.
- **Genericism.** Subject specialists, including those in the curriculum community associated with Christine Counsell, have argued that generic pedagogical frameworks can obscure the way questioning, modelling and practice differ between, say, history and mathematics.
- **Evidence status of the synthesis.** His frameworks (the rainforest, Mode A/B, the 80/20 balance) are professional judgement informed by research, not research findings in themselves. The 80/20 split is a rule of thumb.
- **Mode B.** Some advocates of explicit instruction regard Mode B as a concession to less effective methods; others, such as Kristy Forrest writing in 2020s commentary, argue that Mode B has been under-specified compared with Mode A and needs equally careful codification.
- **Commercial playbooks.** WalkThrus is a paid product and platform; schools should weigh it against building their own shared descriptions of practice.
